                                        BEYOND STATE CAPACITY:
                                        WHY RETRIEVAL BREAKS IN MAMBA

                                         Nir Yarden, Amit Ben-Artzy, and Roy Schwartz
                                         The Hebrew University of Jerusalem
                                         {nir.yarden,amit.benartzy,roy.schwartz1}@mail.huji.ac.il

                                                                                   ABSTRACT

                                                  Mamba models underperform Transformers on in-context retrieval. This gap is
                                                  usually attributed to Mamba’s fixed-size recurrent state, which loses information
                                                  as the context grows. We challenge this assumption by focusing on query-first re-
                                                  trieval, a setup in which the query precedes the context and state capacity should
                                                  not be the binding constraint. We show that even in this setting Mamba lags
                                                  substantially behind Transformers, despite its selection mechanism, which was
                                                  designed to distinguish relevant content from distractors. We then demonstrate
                                                  that the Mamba selection gates have sufficient control over the state updates to
                                                  influence the model output: intervening on specific gates alone can swing accu-
                                                  racy between extremes. However, as gates are input-dependent, they require the
                                                  incoming token’s representation to encode its relevance to the query. We there-
                                                  fore probe token representations across layers, and find that the relevance signal
                                                  emerges only gradually with model depth, and that the gates begin to reflect it
                                                  only several layers deeper. Crucially, we find that gates in retrieval-critical layers
                                                  are not yet aligned with relevance. The selection mechanism is thus expressive
                                                  enough to solve the task, but it commonly operates on a relevance signal that
                                                  has not yet arrived. Mamba’s retrieval bottleneck therefore extends beyond state
                                                  capacity: even when capacity is sufficient, the delayed emergence of relevance
                                                  information across depth often prevents successful retrieval.

                                        1 INTRODUCTION

                                        The Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) has dominated the large language model land-
                                        scape for nearly a decade. Concurrently, the research community has actively sought sub-quadratic
                                        alternatives with competitive performance (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2021), with Mamba
                                        state-space models (Gu & Dao, 2024; Dao & Gu, 2024; Lahoti et al., 2026) emerging as a leading
                                        contender. Mamba’s limitations in long-context retrieval are established in the literature, with prior
                                        work largely attributing them to its fixed-size state capacity: as the state saturates, it inevitably de-
                                        cays and loses information (Jelassi et al., 2024; Waleffe et al., 2024; Arora et al., 2024; Wen et al.,
                                        2025).

                                        State saturation provides a natural explanation for Mamba’s retrieval failures when the model is
                                        forced to store the entire sequence under limited state capacity before discovering what information
                                        will be queried. Providing the query upfront removes this pressure: the model can, in principle,
                                        identify the relevant information as it processes the context and retain only the compact target in-
                                        formation. Much like a human skimming a phonebook for a specific entry, a selective recurrent
                                        model should use its input-dependent gates to filter out irrelevant distractors—precisely the behav-
                                        ior Mamba’s selection mechanism is intended to support. Yet empirical findings show that Mamba’s
                                        retrieval accuracy remains poor even when the query is provided before the context, falling well
                                        behind Transformers (Waleffe et al., 2024). Thus, although state saturation provides an important
                                        explanation for Mamba’s retrieval failures, the poor performance in this setting points to additional
                                        factors at play, motivating us to examine whether Mamba’s selection mechanism can reliably iden-
                                        tify and retain relevant information.

                                        To uncover the root cause of this failure, we work backward from the output to trace where Mamba’s
                                        information flow breaks down (fig. 1). First, we construct a controlled setting using a query-first

key-value retrieval task—concretely, a synthetic phonebook in which the model must return the
value paired with a queried key, embedded among many distractor entries. We find that simply
prepending a relevance flag to the target entry drastically improves Mamba’s accuracy. The flag
explicitly signals the entry’s relevance instead of relying on contextual deduction. We can therefore
localize the bottleneck by comparing information flow between the flagged and unflagged variations.
Specifically, we track the recurrent states to determine when the target information is retained, and
analyze the selection mechanism’s gates (∆) to assess how effectively they distinguish target tokens
from distractors. Next, we probe the target entry token’s representations to measure how deeply its
contextual relevance is encoded and how rapidly this signal reflects upon the selection gates. To
measure the gates’ exact control over performance, we patch gate values between the variants and
inject oracle gate spikes at critical tokens.

Ultimately, we find that the selection gates strictly govern retrieval accuracy: trivial modifications to
these gates can dramatically restore or destroy Mamba’s output. This demonstrates that the selection
mechanism is capable of effective state updates, provided its gates are well-calibrated. The problem,
however, is that the gates are often miscalibrated: long-range contextual information requires many
layers to mix into a token’s representation and then reflect upon its gates. Empirically, critical state
updates tend to occur before the token’s relevance is adequately indicated in the selection mecha-
nism. These findings reveal that limited state capacity alone does not fully account for Mamba’s
retrieval difficulty, and point to selection gate calibration—shaped by both architectural design and
training—as a critical factor in addressing it.

2 BACKGROUND

Built upon the State Space Model (SSM), each Mamba block maintains its own hidden state ht that
recurs across the sequence, taking as input at each position t the token representation xt which is
the previous block output. Within a block, the selective state-space transition is defined as

                      ht = Atht−1 + Btxt,  yt = C⊤t ht                                             (1)

                              At = e∆tA,   Bt = ∆tBt                                               (2)

where At, Bt, and Ct are the state decay, write, and read matrices, respectively, and the selection
gate ∆t is a token-dependent discretization timescale that gates the update of the hidden state: high
∆t nearly resets ht to prioritize xt, while low ∆t discounts xt to preserve state continuity.

Crucially, ∆t, Bt, and Ct are not learned constants but are themselves computed from the current
token representation, via a selection mechanism applied to xt:

          (∆t, Bt, Ct) = selection(xt)  (3)

implemented in practice as a set of linear projections of xt. This makes the state update selective:
every parameter governing how much of xt is written into ht, how quickly the state decays, and how
ht is read out is derived exclusively from xt itself.

The Mamba architecture (Gu & Dao, 2024) is built by stacking Mamba blocks, each combining
this SSM sequence mixing with MLP-like feature transformation, as well as a short convolution—
the resulting block’s output becomes the next block’s xt. This alternation between a token-mixing
operation and a per-token feature transformation mirrors the structure of the standard transformer
block, which stacks attention layers with feed-forward layers. Across layers, this repeated alterna-
tion acts as a pipeline that progressively enriches each token embedding with both linguistic and
context-dependent semantic information (Tenney et al., 2019; Hoang et al., 2025).

3 METHODOLOGY: INFORMATION-FLOW DIAGNOSIS

Experimental strategy To understand the factors underlying Mamba’s retrieval failures beyond
the challenges imposed by its finite state capacity, we need a setting in which the availability of
relevance information can be precisely controlled. We therefore study a query-first retrieval setting
in which a query specifies a target entry in a key-value context, and construct two nearly identical
task variants: one provides only the query, requiring relevance to be inferred from the broader
context, while the other additionally provides an explicit local relevance flag at the target entry.
Because the two variants differ only in the availability of this local relevance flag, the resulting
performance gap provides a direct measure of how much Mamba benefits from having relevance
information available locally, while holding the retrieval problem itself fixed.

Diagnostic framework To understand the source of the query-first retrieval performance gap,
we structure our analysis around the stages of processing that follow from the motivations behind
Mamba’s architectural design. The selection mechanism at each layer is parameterized solely as a
function of that layer’s token representation xt. Thus, for selection to act on relevance, relevance
must first be accumulated in the token representations through the token-mixing process across
layers. We examine how this relevance emerges in the representations, and whether the selection
gates respond to it by favoring target tokens over distractors. Because ∆t jointly governs state writ-
ing and retention, favoring target tokens also requires suppressing updates at subsequent distractor
tokens to preserve the target information in state—an imperative well established in recurrent ar-
chitectures (van der Westhuizen & Lasenby, 2018; Schlag et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2025). We then
consider whether the retained information can ultimately be extracted to produce the output. Across
these stages, we compare the model’s processing with and without the local relevance flag to identify
where its availability changes the model’s behavior.

Datasets We construct a synthetic query-first phonebook dataset based on the reversed setup
of Waleffe et al. (2024), itself introduced in the phonebook task of Jelassi et al. (2024). Each
prompt contains a natural-language description of the task and two in-context examples illustrat-
ing the phonebook format, followed by a phonebook and a request to recall the phone number
associated with a target name. An example is shown in fig. 5. Each phonebook entry follows the
format John Doe: 3948874628\n. We refer to the queried phonebook entry as the target
entry, and to the phone number it contains as the target information. We consider two variants:
in the unflagged dataset, the target entry is indistinguishable from the other entries; in the flagged
dataset, which we introduce, the target entry is explicitly marked, for example, target Jane
Doe: 3827954899\n, providing an immediate local relevance signal.

4 LOCAL RELEVANCE SIGNALS ENHANCE SELECTIVE STATE UPDATES

Waleffe et al. (2024) showed that Mamba underperforms Transformers in query-first retrieval. We
demonstrate that adding a local relevance flag to the target entry substantially improves accuracy,
and we analyze state updates and gating dynamics across both settings to further localize the source
of this failure.

4.1 LOCAL RELEVANCE RESCUES RETRIEVAL

We evaluate Mamba’s accuracy in retrieving phone numbers across the two variants of the phone-
book task, unflagged and flagged, as the number of entries in the phonebook increases. Our results
reveal that the unflagged setup exhibits a trend of gradual performance degradation as context length
grows. In contrast, prepending a local flag to the target entry substantially improves accuracy. While
performance still degrades with longer contexts, the decline is markedly less severe (fig. 2a).

Given that the two variants differ solely by a single word which substantially improves the
performance—more than doubles Nvidia-Mamba’s accuracy on long phonebooks—we conclude
that Mamba processes tokens more effectively when their relevance is indicated locally rather than
established globally hundreds of tokens earlier.

4.2 TARGET INFORMATION IS LESS AVAILABLE IN RECURRENT STATE

Next, we show that the failure stems from improper storage of the target information within recurrent
state updates rather than an inability to extract it at output time. We evaluate how well the target
information is encoded in the recurrent state by capturing snapshots of the state in entries following
the target, then training layer-wise probes (Alain & Bengio, 2017) to predict the target information
from the flattened state. To frame this as a classification task suitable for a linear probe, we modify

the dataset for this specific experiment: each entry consists of a name and a profession drawn from
a closed set of 20 professions, rather than a phone number. We experiment both with the unflagged

Evaluating the probes on the test set (fig. 2b) reveals that target decodability from the recurrent state
is consistently higher on the flagged dataset and emerges at considerably earlier layers compared to
the unflagged dataset. Because state decodability differences between the variants align with accu-
racy differences, this finding directs our remaining experiments toward the mechanisms responsible
for updating the state, rather than those that read from it.

4.3 THE SELECTION GATING STRUGGLES TO DISTINGUISH TARGET FROM DISTRACTORS

We have shown that Mamba’s performance improvement with a local relevance flag stems from
changes in its state updates. Next, we demonstrate that this effect is also reflected in the selection
gates. For this purpose, we evaluate Mamba’s gating mechanism’s ability to distinguish between
target and distractor tokens. Figure 2c shows the difference between the average gate value assigned
to target information digit tokens and distractor digit tokens across layers. The gate value, commonly
denoted dt, is the direct output of the selection projection for the input token1, averaged over all
heads in the layer and samples in the dataset.

Evidently, Mamba’s gates achieve a substantially better distinction between target and distractor
tokens when the target entry includes the local relevance flag. Ultimately, lower accuracy aligns
with inadequate gating in the selection mechanism.

5 SELECTION GATES GOVERN RETRIEVAL ACCURACY

We demonstrated that incorporating a local relevance flag into the target entry substantially improved
retrieval accuracy. This improvement is aligned with enhanced state decodability of the target infor-
mation and a sharper distinction in the selection gates between target and distractor tokens. Next, we
aim to establish a causal link showing that this gating mechanism directly governs model accuracy
in query-first retrieval.

Bidirectional patching transfers performance We test whether the ability of the selection gating
mechanism to separate target and distractor entries causally affects accuracy, beyond the aforemen-
tioned correlation. We run Mamba on each sample’s unflagged and flagged variants to collect both

the baseline accuracy and the selection gate values assigned to the target information tokens. Next,
we run Mamba on the unflagged prompt, patching (Wang et al., 2023) the gate values of its target
information tokens with those captured in the baseline flagged run. Strikingly, this targeted inter-
vention on the selection gates of merely 10 tokens completely shifts the model’s accuracy on the

Oracle scaling demonstrates sufficiency The substantial impact of properly gating target tokens
is further corroborated when, instead of patching the unflagged gate values, we scale them by a
constant factor to spike them—consequently boosting the writing of the target information into the
recurrent state. Figure 3 demonstrates that using the ground-truth indices of the target information
tokens to oracle-scale their selection gates achieves nearly perfect accuracy. Conversely, dampening
the same gates with a scaling factor less than 1 severely degrades performance. This indicates that
well-calibrated gating is a pivotal factor in Mamba’s success.

6 RELEVANCE SIGNALS EMERGE TOO LATE FOR EFFECTIVE RETRIEVAL

We previously established that Mamba’s gating mechanism plays a causal role in its accuracy. In
this section, we show that a token’s relevance signal emerges only gradually across layers, first
becoming decodable in the token’s representation and only later surfacing in the corresponding
gates. Critically, this process is slow enough that by the time the gate reflects a token’s relevance,
layers that play a role in state updates critical for retrieval have often already passed.

6.1 RELEVANCE EMERGES LATE IN TOKEN REPRESENTATIONS

Since the selection mechanism is essentially a single linear layer, we investigate how deep into the
residual stream a token’s relevance signal becomes linearly decodable. For each entry’s final digit
token, we extract its representation at every layer and train layer-wise linear probes to classify target
versus distractor tokens. We report probe performance using AUC, which quantifies the separability
of the two classes.

Figure 4a reveals a substantial difference between the unflagged and flagged datasets in the number
of layers required for the relevance signal to become readily decodable. When Mamba processes a
flagged sample, the relevance signal becomes linearly decodable within one to two residual-stream
updates. In contrast, in the unflagged setting this decodability only plateaus after nearly 10 layers,
indicating that contextual enrichment of the representation is markedly slower when the query is
separated from the target by long-range context.

6.2 GATES LAG BEHIND REPRESENTATIONS

We next test whether relevance decodability in the representation is necessary for well-calibrated
gates, and characterize the resulting dynamics. At each layer, the selection mechanism produces
a gate vector with one entry per head for every token. As in the representation-probing analysis
above, we extract the gate vector at each entry’s final digit token and train layer-wise probes to
classify target versus distractor tokens.

As shown in fig. 4b, the gate vector requires additional layers beyond the representation itself be-
fore relevance becomes decodable. This pattern holds for both the flagged and unflagged datasets:
gate-level decodability rises gradually over roughly 10 layers, beginning near the layer at which the
corresponding representational decodability first emerges. A natural interpretation is that relevance
signals emerge later in the unflagged representations, with this delay affecting the gates, correspond-
ingly pushing the onset of gate-level decodability.

6.3 CRITICAL STATE UPDATES OCCUR BEFORE GATE ALIGNMENT

We extend the gate-patching experiment from section 5 to measure layer-wise dynamics. While the
original experiment patched gates across all N layers simultaneously, we now patch an increasingly
deep prefix of layers (1, 1–2, 1–3, ... , 1–N ) and measure the resulting change in accuracy. This
lets us pinpoint not just whether gate patching causally affects accuracy, but where in the network’s
depth this effect occurs—a result we can connect to our findings on relevance-signal propagation
dynamics. As fig. 4c shows, patching the gates with their flagged counterparts yields small accu-
racy gains that accumulate across a contiguous range of layers as progressively deeper layers are
included. Notably, this effect is concentrated in the early-to-middle portion of the network, rather
than spanning the model’s full depth.

We additionally repeat the experiment, this time patching the token representation rather than the
selection gates. Since the representation is the input of the selection mechanism, patching it indi-
rectly corrects ∆t, Bt, Ct, thereby affecting the entire state update and readout within the layer.
The resulting accuracy pattern closely mirrors that of gate patching alone. This similarity suggests
that within this critical layer range, miscalibrated gating—rather than miscalibration of the other
selection components—is the dominant causal factor limiting accuracy.

Comparing across these experiments, we find that although the relevance signal is already perfectly
decodable in the representation throughout this critical range, the gates fail to reflect it adequately in
the range’s earlier layers—a gap which is consistent with the limited unflagged accuracy. Together,

7 RELATED WORK

Mamba for in-context retrieval Our work investigates query-first retrieval, a setting in which
Mamba’s state-capacity limitations are insufficient to explain performance drops. Prior work has
largely focused on evaluating the structural bottlenecks of recurrent architectures in scenarios where
the query is placed at the end. Both Jelassi et al. (2024) and Waleffe et al. (2024) highlighted
Mamba’s inferior performance relative to Transformers on copying and retrieval, identifying its fixed
state size and fuzzy memory bottleneck as primary causes. Evaluating across complex associative
tasks, Arora et al. (2024) introduced the Multi-Query Associative Recall (MQAR) benchmark, con-
firming that recurrent architectures struggle when multiple associative recalls are required. Formally,
Wen et al. (2025) proved that the maximum context length a recurrent model can reliably retrieve
is bounded linearly with its state size, establishing a hard theoretical limit on Mamba’s retrieval
capabilities.

SSM-Transformer hybrid models While Mamba struggles with in-context retrieval due to its
fixed-capacity nature, Transformers generally perform well on such tasks, which is commonly at-
tributed to self-attention’s ability to directly access individual representations of prior tokens. This
contrast has motivated hybrid architectures (Waleffe et al., 2024; Glorioso et al., 2024; Dong et al.,
2024; Lenz et al., 2025) that interleave SSM and attention layers, using attention to provide pre-
cise token-level retrieval. These approaches have shown promising results and provide an effective
way to mitigate the capacity bottleneck of pure SSMs. Our findings highlight, however, that fixed
state capacity does not fully account for Mamba’s retrieval failures: even in settings where state
capacity is not the limiting factor, Mamba can fail to selectively retrieve information. This suggests
that improving the retrieval mechanisms of SSM layers remains valuable both for pure SSMs and
for hybrid architectures, whose SSM components are still expected to efficiently process and retain
relevant information.

Mechanics of Mamba over long contexts Several recent works localize the components behind
Mamba’s long-context failures. Ben-Kish et al. (2025) attribute degradation to an effective receptive
field dictated by the training sequence length, and use the selection gate as a token-importance score
to compress the context. Bick et al. (2025) compare Transformers and SSMs and find retrieval
carried by a few specialized Gather and Aggregate heads, with SSMs implementing the aggregate
step less sharply. Ye et al. (2025) separate local from global hidden channels and trace long-context
memory decay to the latter. These works ask which component is responsible—which heads, which
channels. We ask instead whether the responsible component has what it needs at the moment it
acts, locating the failure in the timing of information within the network rather than in the identity
or capacity of any component.

Mechanistic interpretability methods Our experimental framework draws upon established di-
agnostic probing and causal intervention frameworks. To inspect internal representations, probing
classifiers (Alain & Bengio, 2017) offer a standard diagnostic tool. However, in light of known
limitations in probing methodologies (Ravichander et al., 2020; Belinkov, 2022), we treat probe
accuracy as correlative, recognizing that extractable information may not be causally used down-
stream. To establish causality, we employ patching—drawing on causal mediation analysis (Vig
et al., 2020) and path patching (Wang et al., 2023)—by replacing a specific component in a forward
pass with its counterpart from a minimally edited counterfactual input. Adhering to activation patch-
ing guidelines (Heimersheim & Nanda, 2024), we apply bidirectional interventions to confirm both
the necessity and sufficiency of these mechanisms.

8 CONCLUSIONS

In-context retrieval is widely recognized as a foundational capability driving core LLM behav-
ior (Olsson et al., 2022; Ram et al., 2023), and the fact that it poses a major difficulty for Mamba
is a key constraint limiting its adoption. This work demonstrated that fixed state capacity is not the
sole mechanistic bottleneck affecting retrieval, by tracing Mamba’s information flow in query-first
retrieval—a setting where large state capacity is unnecessary. By directly intervening on Mamba’s
selection mechanism, we found that naively adjusting the gate values of only a handful of tokens
is enough to make or break retrieval accuracy, and that swapping gate values between two prompts
transfers their outcomes, turning a successful retrieval into a failed one and vice versa. This shows
the selection mechanism is well-suited to govern retrieval accuracy through its control over state
updates, as it is sufficiently expressive to determine the outcome on its own. However, we found
layers critical for retrieval in which the gates are not well calibrated. This is because the relevance
signal only emerges deep into the layer stack, past the point where these gates already need to act
on it.

This work leaves several important open questions. First, the observed performance gap may be
rooted in standard training regimes, which often prioritize local sequence modeling over explicit
global context synthesis, causing a mismatch between the context encoded in token representations
and the inputs required for the selection mechanism to gate successfully. Second, and complemen-
tarily, Mamba’s current design—relying exclusively on a token’s isolated representation to compute
its gate—may introduce a fundamental bottleneck. This forces the model to expend substantial
depth and capacity simply compiling global context into single-token representations—a process
we found completes too late in the network’s depth to influence all decision-critical layers. Future
work exploring alternative inputs to the selection mechanism (Zattra et al., 2025) could help close
the performance gap between SSMs and Transformers in query-first retrieval regimes.

LIMITATIONS

Our empirical evaluation focuses on Mamba-2 models, as open-source Mamba-3 models (Lahoti
et al., 2026) are currently limited to small-scale research checkpoints released by the original au-
thors. To the best of our knowledge, no larger-scale Mamba-3 checkpoints exist anywhere at present.
These smaller parameter scales also lack the capacity to reliably execute the long-context key-value
retrieval task examined in this work. Nonetheless, we conjecture that similar dynamics could persist
in Mamba-3, as its gating design relies on comparable principles. We leave empirical validation on
larger Mamba-3 checkpoints to subsequent work, once such models become available.

Beyond model scope, our analysis relies on a synthetic key-value retrieval task. While simpler than
natural language, the controlled and predictable nature of the task was methodologically necessary,
as it enabled the fine-grained analyses underlying our interpretability experiments. Crucially, key-
value lookup represents a minimal requirement for long-context memory; that Mamba’s selection
mechanism fails even under these idealized conditions points to a fundamental architectural bottle-
neck rather than a task-specific artifact.

A.2 MODELS

Nvidia-Mamba (Waleffe et al., 2024) Model weights are retrieved from the Hugging Face Hub
under the repository nvidia/mamba2-8b-3t-4k. The weights are provided in a Megatron
checkpoint format, which we remap to the official mamba ssm library structure. The model is a
pure Mamba-2 base 8B-parameter language model pretrained on 3 trillion tokens. Architecturally,
it comprises 56 layers with model dimension dmodel = 4096, expansion factor expand = 2, head
dimension dhead = 64 (yielding 128 total heads), state dimension dstate = 128, and ngroups = 8.

Preprint

Codestral-Mamba (Mistral AI, 2024) Model weights are retrieved from the Hugging Face
Hub under the repository mistralai/Mamba-Codestral-7B-v0.1. The model is a 7.3B-
parameter Mamba-2 model instruction-tuned for code generation. Architecturally, it comprises
64 layers with model dimension dmodel = 4096, expansion factor expand = 2, head dimension
dhead = 64 (yielding 128 total heads), state dimension dstate = 128, and ngroups = 8. Presumably
due to Codestral-Mamba’s instruction tuning, its accuracy is consistently higher than that of Nvidia-
Mamba, leading us to evaluate it on longer phonebooks to better amplify its tendencies. Unless stated
otherwise, Nvidia-Mamba is tested on phonebook length of 150 entries, and Codestral-Mamba on
phonebook length of 400 entries.

Tokenization Both models employ SentencePiece tokenizers (Kudo & Richardson, 2018).
Codestral-Mamba uses Mistral AI’s native SentencePiece tokenizer with a 32,768-token vocabu-
lary, whereas Nvidia-Mamba uses NVIDIA’s 256,000-token vocabulary SentencePiece model. Con-
veniently, both tokenizers process phonebook entries in a nearly identical way: name tokens (whose
tokenization can differ) are followed by the separator sequence ": ", 10 individual digit tokens,
and a trailing newline token. This consistent tokenization enables clear alignment between target
and distractor entries, as well as distinct separation between name and digit tokens. Prepending the
word "target" to an entry in the flagged dataset adds merely zero to two tokens to the prompt
sequence. Additionally, all 20 professions in the profession-book dataset were selected such that
each profession is tokenized into a single token across both tokenizers.

Hardware All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA L40S GPU (48 GB VRAM),
except for the state probing experiment (section 4.2), which used two GPUs: one for model inference
and state extraction, and the other for training the probes. Across both models, both datasets, and all
experiments presented in this work, the total compute budget was roughly 1,000 single-GPU hours.

A.3 EXPERIMENTS

Evaluation (section 4.1) For each combination of dataset and phonebook length, model accuracy
is evaluated over 500 dataset samples using a batch size of 1. Correctness is determined by generat-
ing 20 new tokens (top k = 1), extracting a 10-digit sequence via regular expression, and comparing
the extracted sequence against the ground truth. Nvidia-Mamba is evaluated on phonebook lengths
L ∈ {20, 40, . . . , 200}, while Codestral-Mamba is evaluated on lengths L ∈ {20, 40, . . . , 500}.

State probing (section 4.2) For each model and dataset, 15,000 samples are generated and parti-
tioned into an 80/20 train/evaluation split for the probes. Models process inputs using a batch size
of 12. State capture begins immediately after processing the target entry’s profession token and con-
tinues after every subsequent profession token in the prompt, sampling up to 64 states per sample
(evenly spaced along the sequence dimension). States are extracted separately for each model layer
and flattened from their original 3D tensor shape (nheads, dhead, dstate) into a 1D vector of dimension
1,048,576. All states associated with a given sample are assigned strictly to either the training set or
the evaluation set to prevent data leakage. For each layer, we train an independent single-layer linear
probe consisting of 1,048,576 × 20 parameters. The probe weights are initialized in single precision
(fp32) using Kaiming uniform initialization. Probes are trained using the Adam optimizer with a
learning rate of 10−3, zero weight decay, and cross-entropy loss. To avoid writing hidden states to
disk, model forward passes and probe updates are fully interleaved. Extracted states are fed into a
class-balanced experience replay reservoir (128 slots per class), from which probes are trained using
balanced mini-batches of size 64. Probe updates perform 16 training steps after every forward batch
of 12 prompts. Updates begin only after two conditions hold: every class has at least one sample
in the reservoir, and feature-wise mean and standard deviation have been frozen from a warmup of
4,096 captured states. Subsequent training and evaluation both use those frozen statistics to stan-
dardize states. Model evaluation follows the same online pipeline: hidden states are captured during
forward passes and immediately evaluated by the probes, without the 64 states-per-sample limit and
the replay buffer.

Gate distinction (section 4.3) Each model is evaluated using 500 dataset samples. For every sam-
ple, entry, and digit token, we record the gate values across each (layer, head) pair independently
to distinguish the target entry from distractor entries. For each layer, we average these gate values

                                                          14
Preprint

across all heads and samples within each category (target vs. distractor) and compute the difference
targetavg − distractoravg. Consequently, higher positive values indicate stronger model selectivity
distinction between target and distractor entries. Throughout this paper, any experiment referenc-
ing gate values uses ∆t, which is calculated by projecting the layer input xt into an intrinsic value
(commonly denoted as dt), adding a learned bias, and applying a Softplus activation. However, this
specific experiment captures dt instead, as we found that averaging values after applying the activa-
tion function introduces artifacts into the results. This stems from the non-linearity of the Softplus
activation, rather than the gates themselves: averaging post-activation values is not equivalent to
averaging pre-activation values. Other experiments in this work produced equivalent results for dt
and ∆t.

Patching and Scaling (sections 5 and 6.3) For each model, 500 samples are generated and pro-
cessed to evaluate performance and capture intermediate components for activation patching. Each
sample is evaluated in both its flagged and unflagged variants to collect and align the corresponding
internal activations. The patched components analyzed in this work are the selection gates ∆t and
the input representation to each layer xt, which directly influences all downstream selection compo-
nents (including the gates). Along the sequence dimension, activation patching is restricted exclu-
sively to the 10 digit tokens corresponding to the target entry. Along the layer dimension, patching
is applied cumulatively across layers—i.e., iteratively patching layer sets {1}, {1, 2}, . . . , {1, N }—
with accuracy evaluated at each step. fig. 4 presents the results across all cumulative iterations,
whereas fig. 3 shows the final iteration in which all N layers are patched simultaneously. In the scal-
ing experiment (fig. 3(b)), instead of replacing ∆t gates with those from another sample variant, we
multiply the existing gate values by a scaling factor. Note that ∆t, which is obtained using a Soft-
plus activation, is guaranteed to be strictly positive, ensuring that a scaling factor > 1 monotonically
increases the gate magnitude while a scaling factor < 1 has the opposite effect.

Representation probing (section 6.1) For each model and dataset, 500 samples are generated and
partitioned into an 80/20 train/evaluation split for probe training and testing. During model forward
passes, we capture the hidden representations corresponding to the final digit token of each phone
number entry in the phonebook. Representations are collected layer-wise, and an independent probe
is trained for each layer. Each probe consists of dmodel weights (followed by a sigmoid activation)
and outputs a single scalar probability; representations are classified as important using a decision
threshold of 0.5. Probes are trained for 100 epochs over the complete set of extracted representations
using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 1024, a learning rate of 10−3, and zero weight decay.
We optimize a binary cross-entropy loss weighted by the ratio of negative to positive samples to
handle class imbalance. fig. 4 reports the AU C score, which measures how well a classification
model separates positive and negative classes.

Gate probing (section 6.2) For each model and dataset, 500 samples are generated and partitioned
into an 80/20 train/evaluation split for probe training and testing. During model forward passes, we
capture the gate vectors produced by the selection mechanism corresponding to the final digit token
of each phone number entry in the phonebook. Probe training details are completely aligned with
representation probing.
