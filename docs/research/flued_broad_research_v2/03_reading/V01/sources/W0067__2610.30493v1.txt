F EXPERIMENT DETAILS

This appendix describes the data partitions, training procedures, estimators, and numerical optimization used
in the experiments. Section F.1 gives the details for the CelebA experiment, Section F.2 gives the details for the
dSprites experiment, and Section F.3 describes the algorithm used for the Gaussian covariance optimization (9).
The main crossover and recovery figures remain in the main text.

Scope and model selection. We describe the frozen-backbone CelebA experiment and the main dSprites
experiment used in the paper figures. Within each experiment, pretexts share the representation architecture,
optimization settings, data-partition protocol, and rate or regularization grid. Test losses are used for reporting,
not for the checkpoint or hyperparameter selection procedures described below. Architectural dimensions, learn-
ing rates, epoch budgets, and numerical regularization constants are fixed configuration settings. The recorded
experiments do not contain a broader search establishing their optimality. We distinguish these fixed settings
from the explicitly implemented validation-based choices.

Computational resources.  The experiments were conducted on computing infrastructure equipped with four
NVIDIA H100 GPUs.

Consent and sensitive content.  CelebA contains identifiable celebrity face images and appearance-related
attribute annotations. Its official dataset page states that the images were obtained from the Internet, but the
page does not document whether the depicted individuals consented to their inclusion or subsequent research
use. Our study reuses the existing dataset and does not collect new data from participants. The supplement
redistributes no CelebA images, annotations, or per-image feature caches.  dSprites consists of procedurally
generated shapes and contains no human-subject imagery.


F.1  CelebA experiment

Data and frozen features. We sample without replacement 10,000 training images, 2,000 validation images,
and 2,000 test images from the official CelebA partitions of sizes 162,770, 19,867, and 19,962, respectively.
Subsampling uses NumPy generators with seeds 42, 43, and 44 for the three partitions. The validation subset is
randomly divided, using seed 42 + 9182, into 1,000 images for encoder checkpoint selection and 1,000 images for
selection among pretexts. The training and test subsets remain unchanged across the five representation seeds
{42, 43, 44, 45, 46}. These seeds therefore do not constitute five independent data splits.

An ImageNet-1K V1 pretrained ResNet-18, with its final classifier removed, provides 512-dimensional features.
The backbone is frozen and evaluated in inference mode. Anchor images use deterministic bilinear resizing to
a shorter edge of 256, a 224 × 224 center crop, and channel normalization with means (0.485, 0.456, 0.406) and
standard deviations (0.229, 0.224, 0.225). PCA and whitening are fitted exclusively to training anchor features
and retain at most 32 eigen-directions with eigenvalues exceeding max(10−5λmax, 10−12). The fitted transform
is applied unchanged to validation and test images.  Target-view features use separately fitted training-only
PCA/whitening transforms, also of dimension at most 32. Feature extraction uses batches of 64 and two data-
loader workers.

Tasks and pretexts.  The four equally weighted evaluation attributes are Smiling, Eyeglasses, Blond Hair,
and Young. The specialist shown in the figures predicts five disjoint source attributes: Mouth Slightly Open,
High Cheekbones, Wavy Hair, Straight Hair, and Receding Hairline. The generalist reconstructs the anchor
feature vector, rather than image pixels. The complete eight-pretext bank additionally contains lower-region
prediction, grayscale-view prediction, separate expression and hair attribute objectives, an equally weighted
semantic/reconstruction objective, and equally weighted upper/lower-region prediction. The task-aware baseline
uses the four evaluation attributes on the training partition. It is distinct from the source-label pretexts.

The average-task baseline uses a single-target Gaussian pretext with information matrix H = Pt πtHt, formed
from the fitted evaluation-task matrices, as in Theorem 1.  Unlike the task-aware baseline, which optimizes
the weighted mixture of individual task losses, this baseline optimizes the single Gaussian prediction objective
defined by their average information matrix.

Two reproducible target views per configured image policy are cached.  Target augmentations use a random

Gaussian surrogates and channels.  For each scalar attribute, we fit a ridge regression with an intercept
and ridge coefficient 10−3 on whitened training features. Five-fold shuffled cross-fitting, with seed 42, estimates
the residual mean squared error. The final mean predictor is refitted on all training examples. With fitted

folds share the unlabeled PCA/whitening transform fitted on the complete training partition.  Preprocessing
is not refitted separately within each regression fold. Feature-prediction surrogates are obtained from training
cross-covariances and canonical correlations, clipped at 0.99 before conversion to Gaussian signal-to-noise ratios.
Source-attribute surrogates use the same ridge/cross-fitting procedure.

The nominal Gaussian rate grid is {0, 0.25, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32} bits. Given residual covariance K, the channel is
C = AX + ε, with independent standard Gaussian noise and A⊤A = K−1 −I. Pure-noise output directions
are removed. Zero rate is represented by a constant feature. Two independent channel-noise draws per image
are used for probe fitting and scoring. Random-number seeds are shared across candidates and budgets. The
nominal rate is mutual information for the fitted whitened Gaussian model, not an empirical measurement of
mutual information for the non-Gaussian image features.


Finite-code training.  Code budgets are B ∈{0, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32} bits per image. For B > 0, the encoder is a
32 →128 →B multilayer perceptron (with the actual retained PCA dimension replacing 32 if necessary), using

straight-through estimator uses the sigmoid derivative while retaining hard bits in the forward pass. Evaluation
uses deterministic hard bits. Each prediction head has architecture B →128 →m with GELU, where m is
the target dimension. Attribute heads minimize mean binary cross-entropy. Feature heads minimize Gaussian
negative log likelihood per target coordinate, with a learned full residual covariance parameterized by a Cholesky
factor whose diagonal is softplus(a) + 10−3. Composite objectives use separate heads and a shared encoder. At
zero bits, no encoder is trained.

Each nonzero-budget encoder is trained independently for 30 epochs using AdamW, learning rate 10−3, weight
decay 10−4, batch size 256, and gradient-norm clipping at 10. Training shuffles images every epoch and samples
one cached target draw per image and update. The checkpoint minimizing the corresponding hard-code validation
objective, averaged over all cached validation target draws, is retained. This is best-checkpoint selection over a
fixed 30-epoch run, not early termination. The trained task-aware code baseline uses the same procedure and is
not claimed to be globally optimal.


Downstream probes and selection.  Each attribute is evaluated with an independent logistic regression
fitted on representations of the 10,000 training images. An internal randomized 80/20 training partition selects
C ∈{0, 0.1, 1, 10} by binary log loss. C = 0 denotes an intercept-only predictor. The selected model is refitted on
all training representations. Nonconstant logistic models use L-BFGS, tolerance 10−7, and at most 300 iterations.
For stochastic channels, regularization selection uses one draw per training image, followed by refitting on both
draws, avoiding a split of replicas of the same image between fitting and selection. These CelebA probes reuse
representation-training images. Unlike the dSprites experiment, they do not have an independent probe-training
image partition.

averaged across representation seeds. The fixed-pretext rule minimizes that selection loss averaged across budgets

Time complexity.  Let q be the retained dimension, T the number of matrices in one objective, rt = rank(eHt)
after numerical factorization, N the number of outer iterations, and L ≤60 the maximum number of backtracking
trials used in an iteration. Dense symmetric eigendecompositions, matrix-exponential reconstruction, gradient

tasks, an objective/gradient evaluation costs

                                              T          !
                             CH = O X(q2rt + qr2t + r3t )   .
                                                 t=1

evaluation adds O(q3 + CH). Although backtracking requests only an objective value, the value routine also
computes its gradient, so the same CH cost applies to those calls. Consequently, one solve has iteration cost

                         O N(1 + L)(q3 + CH)  ,

or O(N(1 + L)(q3 + Tq2)) for rank-one families and O(N(1 + L)Tq3) in the full-rank case. These are arithmetic-
operation bounds conditional on N and L. The implementation establishes no worst-case bound on N as a
function of the requested gap tolerance.

Preprocessing also matters. Forming the weighted matrix and its relevant-span basis costs O(Td2 + d3).  In
the wrappers, the direct, unoptimized three-operand einsum for U ⊤HtU has contraction count O(Td2q2). A
staged multiplication could reduce this to O(T(d2q + dq2)), but that optimization is not implemented here.
Factorizing the reduced matrices costs O(Tq3). Lifting the solution costs O(dq2 + d2q). These setup operations
are repeated by the main wrappers at each rate rather than cached across the grid. The single-matrix shortcut
costs O(d3 + Jd), where J is the number of scalar root-function evaluations, including dense reconstruction of
K.

Space complexity and repeated solves.  The solver retains dense task matrices even for rank-one families.
Its resident matrix storage is therefore O(Tq2), with factors requiring O(q Pt rt) and a constant number of
q × q work arrays and iterates.  Including the original matrices and lifted covariance in the wrappers gives
O(Td2 + Tq2 + dq) storage for one solve. It would be incorrect to describe the present rank-one implementation
as using only O(Tq) space. Storing solutions for G rates additionally requires O(Gd2) per candidate. The solve
itself does not depend on the number of training images once the task matrices have been formed.  Fitting
features, moments, and neural representations has separate costs. Rate grids and bootstrap replications repeat
the solve, so their work is additive over all candidate/rate/resample combinations, including the corresponding
refitting costs for the moment bootstrap.
