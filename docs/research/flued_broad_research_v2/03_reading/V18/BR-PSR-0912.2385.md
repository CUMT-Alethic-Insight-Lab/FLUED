## Problem, proposed solution, and technique

Partially observed controlled systems need compact states accurate enough for actual planning, not just one-step prediction; latent POMDP learning and prior predictive-state approaches can be inaccurate, locally optimized, or difficult to scale (§1 pp. 1–2). Authors propose spectral identification of a transformed predictive state representation (TPSR), followed by approximate point-based planning on its learned state. The borrowed techniques are observable-operator/subspace identification, singular-value decomposition (SVD), kernel density estimation (KDE), and randomized Perseus value iteration; they are the learner, continuous-observation approximation and planner, not evidence of a neural language mechanism (§§2–4).

## Units, objectives and causality

A history is a sequence of past action–observation pairs. A test is a future action–observation sequence; actions in a test are interventions, not conditioning observations (§2 p. 2). The core predictive state is probabilities of test outcomes conditional on history and specified actions; transformed state is an invertible linear transformation of these predictive statistics, not necessarily coordinate-wise probabilities (§2.1 pp. 3–4). Following each observed action/observation the operator updates and normalizes the state (Eq. 8 p. 4); predicting futures is not access to realized future observations. The objective for the planner is discounted reward, and reward is separately regressed from observed training histories; it is not automatically encoded by compression or an unsupervised reconstruction loss (§4 p. 6, §5.4 p. 8).

## Identification, assumptions and costs

- §§2.1–3, pp. 3–4: finite linear dimension `n`, sufficiently rich tests and indicative history partition so the test/history moment matrix has rank `n`, and `UᵀR` invertible. Estimate history, test/history and action-observation-conditioned moments, choose `n` leading left singular vectors, and use pseudoinverses to identify state/operators up to an invertible transform. Author consistency statement invokes empirical moment convergence and continuity in this finite-rank, nonsingular regime; the paper supplies no general finite-sample guarantee for arbitrary policies. Near-singular moments can make pseudoinversion unstable—conditioning sensitivity is a mathematical qualification, not a measured experiment here.
- §3 p. 4: reset-based sampling assumes histories drawn from a fixed distribution and appropriate intervention trajectories. With one long unresettable sequence, suffix-history sampling assumes a stationary exploration distribution and is explicitly described as only approximate because interventions change later histories. Exploration must cover needed action/history/test events; arbitrary off-support interventions are not established identifiable.
- §3.1 p. 5 generalizes to finite feature sets; rank must survive the feature projections and the indicative features must represent the constant vector. §3.2 pp. 5–6 uses observation kernels, with centers and samples growing and bandwidth shrinking at appropriate rates for its cited KDE limit. Finite estimates can yield negative predicted probabilities; authors suggest thresholding, not an exactness guarantee (§3 p. 4).
- §5.2 pp. 7–8: 10,000 seven-pair trajectories, first 2,000 for centers and other 8,000 for moments; 2,000 history and 2,000 test kernels, 500 observation kernels, six actions, rank 5 selected as smallest producing high-quality policies. The test/history moment matrix is 2,000×2,000; projected action/observation operators avoid constructing every full-size triple matrix. Kernels, moment accumulation, SVD/pseudoinverse, 500 observation operators per action, 8,000 planner belief points, reward regression and iterative planning all count in total cost, not just 5-dimensional deployed state. Timing, memory, energy and equal-compute language-model comparisons are not given in the reported evaluation.

## Observations, comparators, and counterexamples

The single simulated 45×45 arena has colored walls and a central obstacle; input is 16×16 RGB (768 values); six noisy motion actions (§5.1 pp. 6–7). Figure 1 visually relates learned projection to geometry; it is qualitative, and the two displayed axes are not independent proof of all state information. Planning uses reward 1000 for target image and −1 for wall collision, discount 0.8, ten-step horizon, and 8,000 embedded histories (§5.4 p. 8). Figure 2D reports A* 13.9 actions, learned-policy 18.2 **conditional on 78 successes**, and random walk 507.8; 22 of 100 learned-policy trials get stuck alternating actions (§5.4 p. 9). A* has privileged true configuration, so its baseline is not an equally informed policy; the selective 18.2 mean must not be presented as all-trial near-optimality. The authors suggest lookahead/randomization could prevent loops but do not test it in this result. No matched learned-POMDP baseline, diversity across environments, confidence intervals or controlled ablation of state vs planner is reported.

## Direction mapping and transfer boundary

- **B.19**: this is direct evidence that a predictive-state representation can be *estimated* from explored action-observation traces under strong rank/coverage conditions and used for this closed-loop planning example, extending existence-only narratives; it does not establish a language model has learned such a sufficient state.
- **A.06–A.08**: test-set sufficient statistics depend on the modeled dynamical system, choice of features, conditioning and intervention support; unknown query distribution shifts and fixed-byte compression are not evaluated. A small effective state cannot be credited without training/feature/planning resources.
- **E.03**: reward regression and prediction-model learning are staged; no experiment establishes writer/reader co-formation or gradient starvation. Translation to differentiable byte language models, causal memory use, or persistent relational identity is a proposed research question, not an observed result. Distinguish prediction quality from closed-loop success and evaluate failure/coverage under held-out tasks with matched total resources.
