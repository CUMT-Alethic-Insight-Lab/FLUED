                                                                                                       Abstract

                                            Tokenization is an essential preprocessing step in almost all large language models (LLMs). One of the
                                        most popular frameworks is BPE tokenization, which consists of special token segmentation, pretokeniza-
                                        tion, and byte-pair encoding (BPE). However, existing algorithms are sequential, with each stage only
                                        processing complete output from the previous stage. In order to reduce latency and memory footprints,
                                        recent works (Jiang and Gong, ICML 2026) (Li, Yang and Mamouras, ASPLOS 2026) (Mamouras, Li and
                                        Yang, PLDI 2026) have initiated the study of online algorithms for subroutines such as pretokenization
                                        and BPE with a goal of streaming input to output as the input arrives online. However, the search for
                                        efficient online tokenization remains open.

                                            In this work, we formalize and resolve online tokenization. We introduce the notion of delay for online
                                        tokenization which characterizes the extent to which the algorithm output lags behind the (possibly
                                        computationally intractable) optimum. We give a near-linear time algorithm with optimal delay (up to
                                        constant factors). We complement this algorithm with a conditional lower bound: unless P = NP, there
                                        is no polynomial time algorithm that beats the delay of our algorithm by more than a constant factor.

                                            We implement a practical variant for tokenizers from OpenAI’s tiktoken and HuggingFace’s tokenizers.
                                        We obtain a 7.9×-34.8× latency advantage (measured as time to first token) over tiktoken (as well as the
                                        recent gigatoken tokenizer) and our implementation maintains approximately constant memory usage
                                        independent of input size. Our implementation also achieves a 2.4×-7.0× improvement in throughput
                                        over tiktoken and 15.8×-38.1× over tokenizers.

1 Introduction

In many natural language processing (NLP) applications, including powerful and popular large language
models (LLMs), raw text undergoes tokenization before being provided as model input. Tokenization defines
a vocabulary containing as many common words and sequences as possible, which help compress raw text
into tokens, balancing expressivity and computational efficiency.

    Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) is a popular method for tokenization proposed by Gage [Gag94] that has been
widely adopted in LLMs [SHB16]. Indeed, BPE Tokenization is both the subject of intense recent theoretical
study [Bv23; JG26; MLY26] and the basis for popular practical tokenizers, including OpenAI’s tiktoken
and HuggingFace’s tokenizers [Ope26a; Hug26].

    Concretely, tokenization consists of a three step process: Special Token, Pretokenization, and BPE.
As observed in Jiang and Gong [JG26], existing implementations of tokenization are inherently offline and
sequential: we take in the full input, apply segmentation to the full input, then apply pretokenization on each
segment, and then apply BPE to each pretoken. While completed segments and pretokens can be passed
on to the next stage, one should in principle be able to apply pretokenization to a segment as it forms and
similarly apply BPE to a pretoken as it forms. Following this observation, Jiang and Gong [JG26] raise the
question of online tokenization: can we process each stage as the input arrives in an online setting?

    While offline tokenization is now well understood both theoretically and empirically, the question of
efficient online tokenization remains wide open. Online algorithms are desirable for many reasons. First,
we can drastically reduce the the memory footprint of our algorithm by avoiding holding the entire input
or output in memory, a shortcoming of all existing end-to-end tokenization algorithms. Second, streaming
can reduce latency of tokenization, drastically reducing the time to first token. Indeed, streaming and
online algorithms are a fundamental and well-studied paradigm both in theory and in practice, with efficient
algorithms being developed in many applications including counting [Mor78], selection and sorting [MP80],
heavy hitters [MG82], frequency moments [AMS96], and cardinality estimation [FM85; DF03; Fla+07].

    Motivated by latency and memory constraints, online algorithms have been developed for individual
steps of tokenization: Li, Yang, and Mamouras [LYM26] studies the problem of online pretokenization and
Berglund and van der Merwe [Bv23], Jiang and Gong [JG26], and Mamouras, Li, and Yang [MLY26] study
online BPE algorithms. However, none of these works provide an online algorithm for the full tokenization
pipeline. Indeed, Jiang and Gong [JG26] raise the design of end-to-end online tokenization as a crucial
direction for future research. Our work is thus guided by the following question:

                                   Is there an efficient algorithm for online tokenization?

1.1 Formalizing Online Tokenization

We begin with formalizing tokenization which consists of the following three stage process:

   1. Special Token (Seg): Given a list of special tokens S∗ and a string x, split the string by occurrences
       of special tokens (see Definition 4.1) into segments. For example, if S∗ = {"<|endoftext|>"} and the
       input string is x1 = "file1<|endoftext|>file2", we have

                                      Seg(x1; S∗) = "file1" ∥ "<|endoftext|>" ∥ "file2".

       We use ∥ to denote segment boundaries.

   2. Pretokenization (Split): Given a regular expression (regex) R and a string x, split each segment of x
       into pretokens by repeatedly finding the longest match of R in x (see Definition 4.2). For example, if
       R = a∗ | b∗ and x2 = "aaabbba" ∥ "aaaaabbbbaaa",

                                Split(x2; R) = "aaa" ∥ "bbb" ∥ "a" ∥ "aaaaa" ∥ "bbbb" ∥ "aaa".

       As we see in our example, matches cannot extend across segment boundaries.

                                                                      1
   3. BPE (Merge): Given a merge list M and a list of pretokens x, apply BPE to each pretoken of x,
       by repeatedly applying merges in order of priority left to right (see Definition 4.4). For example, if
       x3 = "aaabbbaaa" and M = {(a, b) → X, (a, a) → A, (b, b) → B},

                                                         Merge(x3; M ) = "AXBAa".

    Tokenization, given special tokens S∗, regex R, and merge list M , is defined

                                     Tok(x; S∗, R, M ) = Merge(Split(Seg(x; S∗); R); M ).

Note that each token τ in the output corresponds to a unique string x denoted raw(τ ) where Merge(x) = τ .
For example, using the same R and M as above,

                             Tok(x3; ∅, R, M ) = Merge("aaa" ∥ "bbb" ∥ "aaa") = "AaBbAa".

That is, merges cannot merge across pretoken boundaries, and the merged pretokens are concatenated. In
the offline setting, efficient algorithms are now well known. For example, Seg and Split have classical linear
time algorithms [Rep98]. The naive algorithm for Merge runs in O(nm) time, which is already efficient when
m = |M | is small. When m is large, there are well known O˜(n) time heap-based algorithms (see e.g. the
tiktoken implementation [Ope26a]).

    We now define online Seg, Split, Merge, and Tok. First, for two strings w, w′ in an alphabet Σ, if w is a
prefix of w′, we show it by w ⪯ w′. Consider a function f : Σ∗ → (Σ′)∗ where Σ ⊆ Σ′ are alphabets (e.g.
characters and tokens). In our context, f could be any of the Seg, Split, Merge, or Tok functions. We say
that z ∈ (Σ′)∗ is a stable prefix with respect to x if z ⪯ f (xy) for any y ∈ Σ∗. It is easy to show that stable
prefixes are totally ordered (see Claim 4.8). The maximum stable prefix is then denoted by f⪯(x). We define
online tokenization (analogously each of the three sub-routines) as Tok⪯. In particular, given that input x is
all we have observed so far, online tokenization outputs Tok⪯(x), the longest possible prefix of tokens that
can be safely emitted, regardless of what characters appear in the future. This is exactly the definition that
allows us to stream input characters and output tokens.

    Surprisingly, our first result establishes that online tokenization, unlike its offline counterpart, is NP-
Hard. Given that it is unlikely that even a polynomial (let alone linear time) algorithm for online tokenization
exists, a natural relaxation is to design efficient approximation algorithms. We introduce a natural approxi-
mation of online tokenization which we call delay (see Definition 4.9). An online tokenization with delay k
produces on input x = x1 . . . xn, output z satisfying:

                                                Tok⪯(x1 . . . xn−k) ⪯ z ⪯ Tok⪯(x).

In particular, an algorithm with small delay produces a stable prefix that might not be the maximum stable
prefix, but would have been the maximum stable prefix not too long ago. A trivial algorithm achieves delay
n: output the empty string ϵ. So we focus on answering the following question:

                  What is the minimum delay k with efficient algorithms for online tokenization?

1.2 Our Contributions

We give optimal algorithms for online tokenization, tightly characterizing when efficient online tokenization
is possible, resolving the main open question of Jiang and Gong [JG26]. First, our lower bound rules out the
existence of efficient algorithms with small delay. For any list of special tokens S∗, let ℓ∞(S∗) = maxt∈S∗ |t|
be the longest special token and ℓ1(S∗) = t∈S∗ |t| be the sum of the lengths of the special tokens. For any
merge list M , let m = |M | and t = max {|x| s.t. | Merge(x)| = 1} denote the length of the longest token.

    Note ℓ∞(S∗), ℓ1(S∗), |R| are constants independent of x, so we omit them from bounds for clarity. While
m (and therefore t) is also a constant (since the BPE dictionary is independent of the input string), since m
can be quite large in practice (around 100,000 for CL100K in tiktoken), we will account for m, t in space
and run-time, keeping consistent with prior work [JG26].

                                                                      2
Theorem 1.1 (Hardness of Online Tokenization). Unless P = NP, there is no algorithm for exact (zero-
delay) online tokenization with poly(ℓ1(S∗), |R|, m, t) preprocessing and total time poly(n, ℓ1(S∗), |R|, m, t),
even when S∗ = ∅. Furthermore, the lower bounds hold for algorithms with o(mt) delay.

    A natural attempt at an online tokenization algorithm is to compose online algorithms for special token,
pretokenization, and BPE. We show that this idea of composition does in fact yield an efficient online
tokenization algorithm with non-trivial delay.

    The performance of this algorithm, which we call composition, is parameterized by the following quantity
which depends on the pretokenization regular expression. Given a regular language L, we define the lookback
of the regular language, denoted b(L), to be the smallest integer (or ∞ if no such number exists) such that
every string x ∈ L of length |x| > b has a strict prefix y ≺ x such that y ∈ L, and |y| ⩾ |x| − b (see
Definition 4.3), i.e. y is in the language and y is shorter than x by at most b. For example, if R = a∗, we
have b(R) = 1. On the other hand, if R = a∗b, b(R) = ∞.

Theorem 1.2 (Online Tokenization via Composition). Let S∗ ⊂ Σ∗ be a set of special tokens, R a total
regular expression over Σ, and M a merge list. Then, there is an O(n log2 t) time and O(b(R) + mt) space
algorithm for online tokenization with b(R) + (m + 1)t + ℓ∞(S∗) delay.

Remark 1.3. As part of our algorithm for Theorem 1.2, we design a simple, novel linear time algorithm for
online pretokenization (see Lemma 4.17). In contrast, the previous algorithm of Li, Yang, and Mamouras
[LYM26] incurs preprocessing time and space 2Θ(b(R)). In particular, we achieve online pretokenization
without delay in linear time for all regular expressions under the longest-match convention, whereas the
previous algorithm was only efficient when b(R) = O(log n) is small.

Remark 1.4. While we have efficient algorithms for online BPE [JG26] and pretokenization via Lemma 4.17,
Theorem 1.1 still establishes that online tokenization (with no delay) is NP-hard. Our algorithm for on-
line pretokenization has 0 delay. Known algorithms for online BPE [JG26] have delay O(mt) (see proof of
Lemma 4.18) which suffices for our purposes. We leave as an interesting open question whether zero delay
is achievable for online BPE.

    While our first algorithm does not achieve optimal delay, we show that its space is optimal (up to word
size). In particular, Θ(b(R)) bits is necessary for online tokenization.

Theorem 1.5 (Space Lower Bound). For every integer f , there exists R where b(R) = 2f and online
tokenization requires Ω(b(R)) bits in space.

    While our algorithm requires O(b(R) + mt) space, we note that mt is a constant independent of the input
x, while b(R) can be arbitrarily large (see e.g. R = a∗b above). We note that Theorem 4.24 shows that
Ω(m) bits of space are necessary for online BPE, and therefore online tokenization, essentially showing that
our space usage is optimal (up to a t factor and word size).

    Finally, we discuss delay-optimal algorithms for online tokenization. Note that Theorem 1.2 incurs delay
O(b(R) + mt)1 and thus incurs additional O(b(R)) delay over the lower bound of Theorem 1.1. We show that
this is not an artifact of our analysis: composition alone will always incur delay Ω(b(R)) on some instances.

Theorem 1.6 (Composition Delay Bound). For every integer f ⩾ 2 there exists alphabet Σ, a total regular
expression R over Σ∗, merge list M and some input x where b(R) = f and

                                      Merge⪯(Split⪯(Seg⪯(x))) ≺ Tok⪯(x[: −(f − 2)]).

    Thus, if we want to obtain a delay-optimal algorithm, it cannot be from composition alone. Using these
insights, our main result establishes an algorithm that achieves near linear time, optimal space, and optimal
delay.

    1Note that the size of S∗ is a constant independent of all other parameters. We focus on R and m parameters here as S∗ is
typically not a bottleneck in practice, and we set S∗ = ∅ in the construction of Theorem 1.1

                                                                      3
Theorem 1.7 (Online Tokenization with Constant Delay). Let S∗ ⊂ Σ∗ be a set of special tokens, R a total
regular expression over Σ, and M a merge list. There is an online tokenization algorithm with preprocessing
time O(mt), total time O(n(log2 t + log m + log min(n, b))) and space O(min(n, b) + mt) with O(mt) delay.

    In particular, we characterize (up to constant factors) for what delay efficient online tokenization is
possible. When delay is O(mt), there is a near linear time algorithm. On the other hand, when delay is
o(mt), there is no polynomial time algorithm (unless P = NP).

1.3 Implementation and Experiments

We implement a practical variant and obtain an efficient tokenizer that streams an input file of characters to
an output file of tokens. We evaluate our tokenizer against tiktoken and tokenizers as a drop-in replace-
ment on code (Github), English, and Chinese datasets. See Section 6 for experimental setup, dataset com-
position, as well as implementation details and optimizations. Our code is available at https://github.com/
morganstanley/MSML/tree/main/papers/Online_Tokenization_with_Optimal_Delay_in_Near_Linear_
Time. Our experiments reveal that our implementation exhibits a significant advantage over all evaluated
baselines with respect to latency and memory consumption. We also describe some simple optimizations
that lead to significant throughput advantages over tiktoken and tokenizers.

2 Technical Overview

We now describe the main ideas behind our algorithms.

Efficient Online Pretokenization We begin with Theorem 1.2. Our algorithm is a simple composition
of online segmentation, pretokenization, and BPE. Thus, in order for composition to be efficient, we need
efficient implementations of each online component. Given the efficient online BPE algorithm [JG26], it
suffices for us to design a linear time online pretokenization algorithm with zero delay.

    2Some gigatoken optimizations, such as more aggressive caching of observed pretokens can introduce additional memory
usage that grows with the number and size of distinct pretokens encountered.

                                                                      5
Lemma 2.1 (Online Pretokenization (Informal Lemma 4.17)). There is an algorithm that computes online
pretokenization in O(n) time and O(b(R)) space.

    Given a string x, let F (x) denote the longest prefix of x that matches R. For simplicity, suppose R can
be recognized by a DFA A of size poly(|R|) (in Lemma 4.17 we describe how to handle NFAs). Let Q denote
the set of states and P the accepting states of A. For a string w ∈ Σ+, let δ(q, w) denote the state computed
if we begin at state q and read w, and let last(q, w) be the length of the longest prefix w′ ⪯ w such that
δ(q, w′) ∈ P; i.e. reading w′ from q ends up at an accepting state. A state q ∈ A is dead if there is no
y ∈ Σ+ such that δ(q, y) ∈ P.3

    Consider the following simple algorithm: initialize the variable state to start and on input xi update
state ← δ(state, xi) = δ(start, x⩽i). Whenever state ∈ P, we know that the current prefix x⩽i ⪯ F (x).
That is, x⩽i is a prefix of the longest prefix of x that matches R, so we can emit x⩽i and set last ← i =
last(start, x⩽i). Whenever state ∈ dead, we know that the last accepting prefix (denoted x⩽last) is maximal
and therefore F (x) = x⩽last. Since we have already emitted x⩽last, we can emit ∥ , and restart the algorithm
on input xlast+1.

    We now analyze the performance of this simple algorithm. On each input xi, our algorithm needs
to maintain (1) state = δ(start, x⩽i) to check whether state ∈ P is accepting or state ∈ dead is dead
and (2) last = last(start, x⩽i) to track the longest accepting prefix so far. By restarting the algorithm at
xlast+1, naively scanning the input again requires O(i − last) time. Since every accepting path through the
DFA reaches an accepting state once every b := b(R) steps we have i ⩽ last + b (Lemma 4.20). Thus,
our algorithm backtracks by at most b steps on every output, yielding a running time of O(nb). If we
could instead recompute δ(start, xlast+1 . . . xi) and last(start, xlast+1 . . . xi) efficiently, then we would have an
efficient algorithm for online pretokenization.

    Li, Yang, and Mamouras [LYM26] address this by precomputing δ, last for all possible strings of length
b, resulting in preprocessing time and space that is exponential in b. In this work, we instead give a simple
data structure cQueue that supports exactly the required operations.

Lemma 2.2. There is a data structure cQueue supporting the following operations on a string w:

   1. (Push) Append a character c to w i.e. update w ← wc.

   2. (Pop) Pop a prefix of w, i.e. return w1 . . . wp and update w ← wp+1 . . . wℓ for some p > 0.

   3. (Query) Given a state q, compute δ(q, w) and last(q, w).

Furthermore, the data structure supports the operations in amortized O(1) time.

    Now, to implement the online pretokenization algorithm efficiently, it suffices to observe that the string
xlast+1 . . . xi which we want to query only undergoes queue updates. In particular, we push xi on each input,
and whenever state ∈ dead, we pop up to xlast. Thus, by supporting the required operations in amortized
O(1) time, we obtain a total time O(n) algorithm for online pretokenization.

Composition Delay Bound We now argue that composition already achieves non-trivial delay O(b+mt).
Composition always outputs a stable prefix (and this in fact holds for composition of any two functions f, g
see Claim 4.19). If x′ ⪯ g(xy) for any continuation y, and z ⪯ f (x′y′) for any continuation y′, then
z ⪯ f (g(xy)) for any continuation y, since g(xy) = x′y′ for some y′.

    To bound the delay, it suffices to bound |x| − |raw(z)| where z is the output of our algorithm (see
Claim 4.21). Let z1 = Seg⪯(x). In Lemma 4.16, we show |raw(z1)| ⩾ |x| − ℓ∞(S∗) where ℓ∞(S∗) =
maxτ∈S∗ |τ | is the length of the longest special token. Now, let z2 = Split⪯(z1). As discussed in the
pretokenization algorithm, there are two cases when we can safely emit a stable prefix of Split: either
state ∈ P and we can emit the accepted prefix, or state ∈ dead and we can emit ∥ and rewind to the
last accept. Since the DFA must reach a P state every b = b(R) steps, Split⪯ outputs all but the last b(R)

|raw(Merge⪯(x))| ⩾ |x| − (m + 1)t.4                                                                    (1)

Limitations of Composition While composition produces a stable prefix with non-trivial delay, it falls
short of an optimal delay algorithm for online tokenization. While the additive ℓ∞(S∗) term is an input-
independent constant (and usually not a large one in practice), the additive b(R) term can be arbitrarily
large. Theorem 1.6 shows that this delay is inherent to composition.

    Consider the following simple example of a tokenizer: R = anb | a | b and M = [bb → B]; and an input
x = an. On the one hand, b(R) = n and indeed Split⪯(x) = a, since two possible continuations produce
Split(x) = a ∥ . . . ∥ a and Split(xb) = anb with a longest common prefix of a. On the other hand, since no
pretoken contains bb, then regardless of the pretokenization, no merge ever occurs. For our specific example,
we in fact have Tok⪯(y) = y for any input y. Concretely, we argue the delay is greater than n − 2 as the
algorithm outputs a and

                                 Tok⪯(x[: −(n − 2)]) = Tok⪯(x⩽2) = Tok⪯(aa) = aa ̸⪯ a.

    From the above example, we observe that composition will always produce an output P where raw(P ) ⪯
x⩽last, where x⩽last is the longest accepting prefix in the pretokenization. However, a zero-delay online
tokenization algorithm might produce output P ′ that is strictly longer than ⩽last, i.e. x⩽last ≺ P ′. This
is because different continuations may produce different pretokenization outputs, but they nevertheless can
produce the same tokenization at the end.

Optimal Delay Algorithm We are now ready to describe our algorithm with optimal delay. Suppose
our algorithm proceeds inductively, beginning with an empty output which is a stable prefix of all possible
outputs. Consider some stable prefix P of the online tokenization output, i.e. P ⪯ Tok⪯(x) which we have
computed so far. Since Tok⪯(x) ⪯ Tok(x), any stable prefix P ′ ⪯ Tok⪯(x) must be a prefix of Tok(x). Let
θ be the token following P in Tok(x), i.e. P θ ⪯ Tok(x). Then, P is not a maximum stable prefix if and
only if P θ ⪯ Tok⪯(x): θ is the only candidate token to extend P to a longer stable prefix. It thus suffices to
decide whether P θ ⪯ Tok⪯(x), equivalently, whether for all y ∈ Σ+ we have P θ ⪯ Tok(xy).

    Recall that last = last(start, x) is the index of the last accepting state in the pretokenization DFA. Denote
Split(x) = z1 ∥ . . . ∥ zp so that z1 = x1 . . . xlast5 and

                                      Tok(x) = Merge(z1) ◦ Merge(z2) ◦ · · · ◦ Merge(zp).

Let out be the index of the last output character so that raw(P ) = x1 . . . xout. Let last(0) = 0 and 1 ⩽ q ⩽ p,
let last(q) be the index such that zq = xlast(q−1)+1 . . . xlast(q). Note that last = last(1).

state = δ(start, x) ∉ dead. First, if i − out ⩽ 3mt, then we already have that P has delay O(mt). Thus,
assume i − out > 3mt.

    To implement the above algorithm efficiently, we need a few additional data structures. First, we need

the next token in the event that a future continuation extends the current pretoken match. To support these
operations efficiently, we implement two useful data structures. Note that both data structures support
pushing string characters and ∥ to denote the beginning of a new pretoken (pushing ∥ does not contribute
to the length of the maintained string).

Lemma 2.3 (Incremental BPE). Given a BPE merge list M with longest token length t, there is a data
structure incBPE that maintains implicitly an append-only history string h, an index 0 ⩽ I ⩽ |h|, and
explicitly string w = h>I under the following operations:

   1. (Push) append c to h ← hc. Note that we can push ∥ to denote a pretoken split.

   2. (Pop) if I < |h|, increment I by 1.

   3. (First Token) if Merge(h⩽I ) ≺ Merge(h), compute ϕ(w). Otherwise return ⊥.
The data structure has O(mt) preprocessing, space O(mt + |w|), worst-case update time O(log2 t + log m) and
query time O(log t + log |w|).

    The second data structure bpeQ allows us to compute τ efficiently.

Lemma 2.4 (BPE Queue). Given a BPE merge list M , there is a data structure bpeQ supporting the
following operations on a string w:

   1. (Push) Append a character c to w i.e. update w ← wc.

   2. (Pop) Pop w1, i.e. return w1 and update w ← w2 . . . wℓ.

   3. (First Token) If |w| ⩾ 3mt, then compute ϕ(w). Otherwise, return ⊥.

Furthermore, the data structure has O(mt) preprocessing, O(mt+|w|) space, and supports updates and queries
in amortized time O(log2 t).

last(q − 1) ⩽ out < last(q). We note that last(q) is monotonically increasing: since we are always looking for
the longest match, additional characters can only extend a pretoken.

Hardness of Exact Online Tokenization Our algorithm does not achieve exact online tokenization
(zero delay), and we give a lower bound ruling out any polynomial algorithm unless P = NP. We will
reduce 3-SAT to online tokenization. Consider 3-SAT on d variables. First, we construct an alphabet Σ,
regular expression R, and merge list M depending only on d. We will ensure that the alphabet size, the size
of a DFA recognizing the regular expression, and the length of the merge list all have size poly(d). Then,
we give a reduction from any d-variable 3-SAT instance ψ to input strings zψ of length poly(d) such that
there is a specific alphabet character α ∈ Σ that appears in Tok⪯(x) if and only if ψ is unsatisfiable (see
Theorem 1.1 for details and proof with delay).

define a symbol ℓc. Define ΣC = {ℓc}c∈C. Define the alphabet

                                      Σ = a10, a11, b1, . . . , ad0, ad1, bd ∪ {α, β, γ, δ} ∪ ΣC.

sion Rc = ℓcΣ∗CαβEcγ.

    To define the tokenization, fix S∗ = ∅ and R = RC | αβEγδ | Σ where RC is the alternation of all Rc, E

merge rules will be chosen so that within any pretoken, α is merged if and only if the suffix of the pretoken
beginning with α has even length. Observe that this occurs if and only if α occurs in the pretoken αβEγδ
(since Σ, RC both have odd length suffixes beginning with α). Then, given a 3-SAT instance ψ = c1 ∧ · · · ∧ ck,
construct the input z = ℓc1 . . . ℓck α.

Regular Expressions The regular languages over an alphabet Σ consist of the smallest collection R =
R(Σ) of languages L ⊆ Σ∗ that satisfy the following rules:

   1. ∅ ∈ R, {ϵ} ∈ R, and {σ} ∈ R for all σ ∈ Σ.

   2. (Concatenation) If L1, L2 ∈ R, then L1 ◦ L2 = {x1 ◦ x2 | x1 ∈ L1, x2 ∈ L2} ∈ R.

   3. (Alternation) If L1, L2 ∈ R, then L1 | L2 = L1 ∪ L2 ∈ R.

   4. (Star) If L ∈ R, then L∗ ∈ R.

When convenient, we use L1L2 to denote L1 ◦ L2. A regular expression R is total if every non-empty
x ∈ Σ∗ has a non-empty prefix matched by R (possibly including x itself). Note that this implies Σ ⊆ L(R).
Throughout this work, we consider only total regular expressions.

    Regular languages are closed under complement, union and finite intersection. For our theoretical results,
a match of regular expression R in x is the longest prefix of x that matches R, or equivalently the longest
prefix of x in L(R). Since we assume R is total, this is a non-empty prefix.

    For a total regular expression R and non-empty input x, define the longest match of R in x, denoted
F (x; R) as x⩽J(x;R) where J(x; R) = max {j > 0 s.t. x⩽j ∈ L(R)}. When the context is clear, we simply
write F (x), J(x). We note that for any regular expression R, there is a non-deterministic finite automata
(NFA) that recognizes exactly L(R) of size O(|R|) (see e.g. Aho et al. [Aho+06]). In Section 6, we will
separately discuss examples of regular expressions used in practice, which use regular expressions with
priority rules and lookahead.

    Given a DFA or an NFA A, let Q be its states. Let P ⊆ Q be the accepting states, and dead ⊆ Q be
the set of states that have no non-trivial path to a state in P. Note that a dead state can be a P state,
since we do not count the trivial transition on the empty string ϵ. For our purposes of simulating a regular
expression with a DFA, if a state is dead and in P, it is a maximal match. Let start denote the start state.
For a state q and word w, δ(q, w) denotes the state computed if we start on q and read w. last(q, w) denotes
the maximum i where δ(q, w⩽i) ∈ P, or ⊥ if no such i exists. For an NFA, we let Q be a set of states and
δ(Q, w) be the set of states reachable if starting at any q ∈ Q and reading w and last(Q, w) the maximum i
where δ(Q, w⩽i) ∩ P ≠ ∅, or ⊥ if no such i exists.

a unique greatest element z∗ under ⪯, and every stable prefix is a prefix of z∗.

Proof. We begin with the following fact: if z ⪯ L and u ⪯ L, then z ⪯ u or u ⪯ z. Assume without loss of
generality |z| ≤ |u|. Since both are prefixes of L and |z| ≤ |u| ≤ |L|, we have z ⪯ u.

    Define Tok(x) := Tok(x; S∗, R, M ). Then, since z1, z2 ∈ S(x), we have z1, z2 ⪯ Tok(x) so that either
z1 ⪯ z2 or z2 ⪯ z1. Thus, ⪯ is in fact a total order, so that there exists a unique greatest element z∗.

it is updated only by appending new characters. An algorithm outputs y incrementally if it outputs by

4.1 Exact Online Tokenization

We begin with a conditional lower bound showing that it is likely hard to design an efficient algorithm for
online tokenization without delay.

Theorem 1.1 (Hardness of Online Tokenization). Unless P = NP, there is no algorithm for exact (zero-

even when S∗ = ∅. Furthermore, the lower bounds hold for algorithms with o(mt) delay.

4.2 Online Tokenization via Composition

Given Theorem 1.1, our goal is to design fast algorithms with minimal delay. In particular, we would like
to design algorithms running in almost linear time (e.g. n1+o(1)). We begin with a simple algorithm that
composes online segmentation, pretokenization, and BPE merging.

    We first define online segmentation, pretokenization, and BPE merging. Note that Claim 4.8 and
Claim 4.10 do not use anything specific about the tokenization process, and can be shown for any func-
tion that takes an (incremental) list as input and outputs an (incremental) list. Thus, again in principle, we
can hope for efficient online segmentation, pretokenization, and BPE merging. Since pretokenization takes
a list of lists as an input and outputs a list of strings, we say a list of strings is updated incrementally if it

                                                                  16
is updated by either appending characters to the current last string, or starting a new string. Similarly, an
algorithm outputs a list of strings incrementally by either appending characters to the current last string,
or starting a new string. We let SSeg(x; S∗), SSplit(L; R), and SMerge(L; M ) denote the set of stable prefixes
with respect to S∗, R, M respectively, where x is a string input to segment with S∗, and L is a list of strings
input to pretokenization or BPE.

Definition 4.13 (Online Segmentation). Let S∗ ⊂ Σ∗ be a set of special tokens. Online Segmentation, given
an incrementally updated string x, incrementally outputs Seg⪯(x; S∗) = max⪯ SSeg(x; S∗).

Definition 4.14 (Online Pretokenization). Let R be a total regular expression over Σ. Online Pretokeniza-
tion, given an incrementally updated list of strings L, incrementally outputs Split⪯(L; R) = max⪯ SSplit(L; R).

Definition 4.15 (Online Byte-Pair Encoding). Let M be a BPE encoding. Online BPE, given an incre-
mentally updated list of strings L, incrementally outputs Merge⪯(L; M ) = max⪯ SMerge(L; M ).

    As before, we omit S∗, R, M when convenient. We observe that for a list L = a1 ∥ . . . ∥ as, Split⪯(L) =
Split(a1) ∥ . . . ∥ Split(as−1) ∥ Split⪯(as). Indeed, any continuation of L has the first s−1 segments identical
to L and the s-th segment is a continuation of as. Similarly, Merge⪯(L) = Merge(a1) ◦ · · · ◦ Merge(as−1) ◦
Merge⪯(as).

    We give a linear time algorithm for online tokenization.

Theorem 1.2 (Online Tokenization via Composition). Let S∗ ⊂ Σ∗ be a set of special tokens, R a total
regular expression over Σ, and M a merge list. Then, there is an O(n log2 t) time and O(b(R) + mt) space

5 Improved Delay Beyond Composition

We show that a more careful algorithm can achieve delay O(mt) independent of b. Theorem 1.1 establishes
that this delay is optimal for polynomial time algorithms. We begin with some notation and useful properties.
Recall that for any string x, ϕ(x) is the first token in Merge(x).

Claim 5.1 (Theorem 2 of Berglund and van der Merwe [Bv23]). Let M be a merge list with m pairs and t
the length of the longest token. For any two input strings x, y, if x⩽(m+1)t = y⩽(m+1)t, then ϕ(x) = ϕ(y).

    Throughout this section, we will view BPE as the following process on an input string x. Every pair
of adjacent characters initially has a cut between them, that is, every character begins as a single token.
Pretokenization fixes certain cuts in place (i.e. the cuts that represent cuts between pretokens denoted by
∥ ). For example, the list of pretokens

                                                       "aaa" ∥ "bbb" ∥ "aaaa"

can be viewed as list of characters with cuts

                                                   a|a|a∥b|b|b∥a|a|a|a

where ∥ represents fixed cuts given by the pretokenization phase and | represents unfixed cuts (or simply
cuts) that can be removed by the BPE merge process. BPE merge on each pretoken then removes cuts,
i.e. every merge removes a cut between two adjacent tokens. Following the above example, the merge rule
(a, a) → A removes cuts so that the output is

                                                       aa | a ∥ b | b | b ∥ aa | aa

corresponding to the output string "AabbbAA".
    An ordered pair of tokens (τ, µ) is consistent, denoted τ → µ, if

                                                    Merge(raw(τ )raw(µ)) = τ µ.

We show that consistency extends to sequences of tokens.

                                                                     29
Claim 5.2. Let τ1, . . . , τk be a sequence of tokens. Then

                            Merge(raw(τ1) . . . raw(τk)) = τ1 . . . τk ⇐⇒ τi → τi+1 ∀ 1 ⩽ i < k.

We say τ1, . . . , τk is a consistent sequence of tokens if they satisfy the left hand side. Consequently, for any
token µ and consistent sequence of tokens τ1, . . . , τk, we have µ, τ1, . . . , τk is consistent iff µ → τ1.

    Following the above convention of viewing BPE as a process of removing cuts between characters, a
sequence of tokens τ1 . . . τk can be viewed as the raw string raw(τ1) . . . raw(τk) with the cuts surviving the
BPE merge process exactly denoting the cuts in the final output.

                                               raw(τ1) |1 raw(τ2) |2 · · · |k−1 raw(τk)

where |i denotes the surviving cut between token τ1 and τ2.

Proof of Claim 5.2. Suppose τ1, . . . , τk is consistent. Consider the interval raw(τi)raw(τi+1) for any 1 ⩽
i < k with endpoint cuts |i−1, |i+1. Observe that for two strings containing this interval, if after applying
merge m ∈ M , neither endpoint cut is merged, then the cuts internal to the interval are identical after
applying merge m. Then, consider the strings raw(τ1) . . . raw(τk) and raw(τi)raw(τi+1). Since τ1, . . . , τk is
consistent, the endpoint cuts |i−1, |i+1 are never merged. The same statement holds trivially over the string
raw(τi)raw(τi+1). Thus, since the cuts internal to the interval are identical, we have that τi → τi+1.

    Conversely, suppose every pair τi → τi+1 is consistent. If every cut between τi, τi+1 is not merged, then
we have Merge(raw(τ1) . . . raw(τk)) = τ1 . . . τk by the above argument. Thus, assume some cut between
τi, τi+1 is merged. Let m be the highest priority rule that removes a boundary cut, and among the boundary
cuts removed by m, let |i be the one of minimum index. We claim that at the instant |i is removed, neither
endpoint cut |i−1 nor |i+1 has been removed. Indeed, by the choice of m, no rule of higher priority than m
removes a boundary cut, and every rule of lower priority is applied only after m; so it suffices to rule out
that m itself removes an endpoint cut before |i. For |i−1, this holds by the minimality of i. For |i+1, note
that minimality does not apply, since i + 1 > i; instead, if m also removes |i+1, then it does so only after
removing |i, as |i lies to the left of |i+1. Consequently, the cuts internal to the interval raw(τi)raw(τi+1)
are identical to those in the Merge process on raw(τi)raw(τi+1) alone, so |i is removed there as well. This
contradicts τi → τi+1.

Proof of Lemma 2.4. We require a modification of the incremental algorithm of Jiang and Gong [JG26].

Lemma 5.3. Given BPE merge list M , there is an algorithm Φ with O(mt) preprocessing and space that

    Finally, we can use a standard trick to convert the batch algorithm into an algorithm with efficient
amortized update and query time. We maintain two queues U and V such that the maintained string w
satisfies w = U V . Initially, both U, V = ϵ. We maintain the invariant that:

On a push, we push the character to V . On a pop, we pop the character from U if it is not empty. Otherwise,
V is not empty and we remove a character from V . After each operation, if |U | < 2mt and |w| ⩾ 3mt, then
we have |V | > mt, in which case we let U ← w⩽3mt and apply the algorithm of Lemma 5.3 to U .

    We check that the invariant is always maintained. Consider the first time |w| ⩾ 3mt, at this time we have
|V | = 3mt and U is empty. Our algorithm detects this, and moves w to U , applying the batch algorithm to
U , maintaining the invariant. Subsequently, anytime |U | < 2mt and |w| ⩾ 3mt, we again transfer w⩽3mt to U
and apply the batch algorithm. Note that |U | ⩽ 3mt always and ϕ(U⩾i) is always computed, since whenever
we add elements to U , we recompute ϕ(U⩾i) on the whole object U , while popping elements from U ensures
that ϕ(U⩾i) is still computed on the remaining elements. Each application of the batch algorithm requires
O(mt log2 t) time and ends with |U | = 3mt. Since we do not apply the algorithm again until |U | < 2mt, or
after Ω(mt) operations, the amortized time is O(log2 t).

    Finally, correctness follows since whenever |w| ⩾ 3mt, the invariant guarantees |U | ⩾ 2mt. Claim 5.1
then ensures that ϕ(w) = ϕ(U ).

   1. r"'(?i:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)" matches common apostrophed suffixes such as "'ll".

   8. r"\s+" and r"\s" matches consecutive whitespace and single whitespace respectively.

    In these reference patterns, matching is computed in order of priority, then length. In particular, given a
regular expression R = R1 | R2 | · · · | Ri the first match of R in x, denoted F ′(x; R) is defined as x⩽J′(x;R)
where

           J′(x; R) = max {j > 0 | x⩽j ∈ L(Ri) and no non-empty prefix of x is in L(Rk) for k < i} .

That is, instead of just taking the longest prefix that matches any rule, we take the highest priority rule
that has any match with a prefix of x, and the longest match under that rule. We observe that given the
left-to-right priority of the alternation rules in the regex pattern, a string is accepted by a rule if (1) it
matches the pattern of that rule and (2) it has no prefix that matches any higher priority rule. Note that
(online) pretokenization (Definition 4.2) is defined identically, replacing F with Fref .

    Beyond left-to-right priorities, practical patterns also implement the lookahead operations. In particular,
CL100K and O200K contain the rule r"\s+(?!\S)" which matches runs of whitespace that are followed by
whitespace or end of input (but not runs of whitespace followed by a non-whitespace character). In this case,
a DFA may need to scan ahead by one character to accept a prefix. For example, if x = "\t\t\ta", the
DFA must scan "\t\t\t" before accepting "\t\t". We note that even with the priority rules and lookahead
operation the CL100K and O200K examples can still be represented with simple DFAs (see Section A). In
our examples, the DFA needs to scan ahead by at most one character.

    With both priority and lookahead, let Fref (x; R) be the prefix of x matched by R incorporating all priority
and lookahead rules. Define Jref (x; R) = |Fref (x; R)|.

    In view of priority and lookahead, we give a generalized definition of lookback (Definition 4.3).

Definition 6.1. A regular expression R has bref -generalized-lookback if for any x ∈ Σ∗ and a ∈ Σ, |Jref (xa)−
Jref (x)| ⩽ bref .

Jref (x)| = J(xa) − J(x) ⩾ 0 is only non-zero when xa ∈ L, in which case J(x) is exactly the longest match
among strict prefixes, so that bref (R) = b(R).

    For the reference patterns considered here, we adapt the simple backtracking algorithm of Lemma 4.16
to compute pretokenization on a pattern with priority rules and one character lookahead. When bref (R)
is finite, a similar proof can show that the structure of the DFAs ensure that each restart reprocesses only
O(bref (R)) characters. Charging each restart to progress through the input gives O(nbref (R)) total time. We
use this approach in practice because it is simple and avoids the overhead of the more general data structure
in Lemma 4.17. We further justify this choice by showing that in most practical cases, bref (R) is small.

Claim 6.2. The R50K pattern satisfies bref (R50K) ⩽ 2.

Proof of Claim 6.2. Appending a character can extend an existing run of letters, digits, or symbols by at
1. For runs of letters and numbers, appending a character cannot lead to a match with a higher priority

6.2 Optimization

We now describe some optimizations in our code to improve the practical efficiency of our algorithm.

Chunk Size We read input in fixed-size chunks to amortize input overhead, observing that chunks of
64KiB (or larger) are most efficient in practice. The read chunk size controls input batching. For each
chunk, the unresolved suffix may contain a candidate pretoken that is retained until it is resolved by reading
the next chunk.

Pretokenization Algorithm We remark that in practice, the simple backtracking algorithm for online
pretokenization that requires O(nb) time is more efficient than the O(n) time algorithm given by Lemma 4.17.
In particular, the overhead of the linear-time algorithm depends on the size of the regular expression, which
is typically larger than the overhead incurred by backtracking (since in practice b is often very small, see
e.g. Claim 6.2).

                                                                     40
BPE Algorithm. To favor throughput, our implementation invokes the incremental (but not online)
BPE algorithm of Jiang and Gong [JG26], which only merges after a pretoken boundary is resolved. This is
consistent with Jiang and Gong [JG26], which observe that the online BPE algorithm is slower in practice.
The implementation therefore differs here from the theoretical composition algorithm, which emits accepted
prefixes of candidate pretokens to an online BPE merging algorithm. The practical implementation can
consequently (in the worst case) retain an arbitrarily long unfinished pretoken and defer applying BPE to
merge this pretoken until the pretoken is complete. Its worst-case memory and latency can therefore depend
on pretoken length, even for patterns with bounded lookback. We note that for the implemented regular
expressions, this scenario typically does not occur on natural language, evidenced by the memory and latency
advantages of our implementation.

Linear Buffering Suppose that we have read in a chunk into a buffer, and our online tokenization algo-
rithm has decided that some small prefix of the chunk can be stably tokenized and emitted as output. We
now need to remove this prefix on the chunk and recurse the tokenizer on the remaining suffix. Naively
draining the buffer (e.g. std::String::drain) after each token is emitted requires linear time, since we
shift the entire array of characters forwards. Since tokens are typically much shorter than a chunk, this
requires time that is quadratic in the chunk size. Instead, whenever we emit a token, we simply advance a
counter on the buffer to mark which characters have been successfully processed. At the arrival of a new
chunk, we keep the last trailing token (typically quite short) and drain the processed prefix in time linear in
the chunk size.

DFA Implementation A key optimization of our code is a faster implementation of the regular expression
matching required by pretokenization. Indeed, regex parsing is the dominant cost of tokenization (see OpenAI
[Ope26b]). Instead of directly implementing the pretokenization patterns (e.g. R50K, CL100K) as a DFA, we
use a combination of (1) a simpler pattern that can be efficiently implemented as a DFA, and (2) an auxiliary
ApplyFixup function that ensures our algorithm exhibits identical behavior to a DFA directly implemented
on the original pattern.

    We note that the choice of DFA implementation (either the lazy DFA or dense DFA in regex_automata)
once the pattern is fixed does not lead to any significant differences in performance. Furthermore, since we
work exclusively with total regular expressions, we use anchored DFAs, which ensure that the first character
is matched (removing the overhead caused by searching for alternative starting characters). In Section 6.3,
we present theoretical and empirical evidence that our alternative implementation is in fact equivalent to a
direct implementation of the DFA.

Segmenting Finally, we optimize the special token segmenting. Since special tokens typically start with
the same character (e.g. <) and this character is relatively rare in natural text, we use memchr [Bur26], an
optimized library for string search in Rust to efficiently find instances of the starting characters.

Proof of Claim 6.6. We begin with a brief refresher on possessive quantifiers (see e.g. Oracle [Ora24] and
Regular-Expressions.info [Reg25]). Given a pattern r"A++B", a possessive quantifier tries to (1) find the
longest match satisfying r"A+", and (2) the longest match of r"B" in the remaining string. If no such match
exists, then the possessive quantifier reports that no match is found. In contrast, if no such match exists,
the greedy quantifier given by r"A+B" backtracks to the next longest prefix matching r"A+", and attempts
to match r"B" from there. As an example, r".++x" would fail to match "abcx", since (1) matches the whole
string leaving no characters for (2), whereas r".+x" would find a match by backtracking one character.

    Suppose for contradiction that Fref (x; R1) ≠ Fref (x; R2) for some x. Let a1 = Fref (x; r"A+") be the
longest prefix match of r"A+" in x. Then, there is no match for r"B" in x \ a1. On the other hand, there is
a prefix a2 ≺ a1 such that x \ a2 contains a match for r"B". In particular, a prefix of x \ a2 is a suffix of a1,
a contradiction since r"A" and r"B" are over disjoint sets of literals.

    Claim 6.6 applies to the possessive symbol run followed by the disjoint newline class in the fourth rule. In
the second rule, the optional possessive prefix excludes letters, so giving back its character cannot enable the
following letter match. The bounded digit pattern in the third rule and the remaining possessive quantifiers in
these three rules occur at the ends of their rules, where no subsequent sub-pattern can require backtracking.
Thus removing the possessive modifiers preserves the matches of the second, third, and fourth rules.

the entire input string is all horizontal whitespace followed by EOF, so that neither extend or trim trigger,
and we return the full matched string, as desired.

the fifth rule, extend will not trigger since the remaining string is not all whitespace. Since xj is not
horizontal whitespace, trim does not trigger either.

afterwards since the fifth rule did not match). In this case, we match the last rule of the simplified pattern
(which matches all runs of horizontal whitespace). Since we matched also the trailing horizontal whitespace

w without its final character. The simplified pattern matches all of w, and trim removes its final character,
yielding the same match.

    Finally, suppose the original pattern matches the seventh rule. Since the sixth rule did not match, the
initial run consists of a single horizontal whitespace character followed by a non-whitespace character. The
simplified pattern matches this same character, and trim does not trigger because the candidate has length
one. This concludes the proof.

    Next, we verify the correctness of our algorithm empirically by enforcing equal output with reference
tokenizers on test datasets.

Empirical Validation: Equivalence on Datasets Finally, we ensure that our algorithm’s output is
byte-identical to tiktoken and tokenizers on the three given datasets (English, Chinese, and Github). We
also empirically verify that our implementation is byte-identical to tiktoken and tokenizers on a large
number of short strings.

We now present the results of our empirical evaluations.

7.1 Time to First Token

Next, we measure the time to first token (TTFT), a key metric in streaming and online applications. For
each tiktoken encoding and dataset, we ignore encoder construction time, and measure the time from when
the input is provided to the encoder to when the first token is formed. Note that we measure the time to
when the first token is formed, not when it is output, since both tiktoken and gigatoken do not output any
tokens until all tokens are formed. For our tokenizer, we provide the input as a file, so that input-reading
time is included. For tiktoken and gigatoken, we provide the string as input in memory, thus excluding
input-reading time. We note that tiktoken and gigatoken do not natively provide support for TTFT
profiling: we make minimal changes to their code to allow for this measurement, as the alternative simpler
method of measuring TTFT as the total time of encoding excessively advantages our algorithm (see code
for details). TTFT results are provided in Table 3.

7.2 Memory

Next, we examine the memory required by each tokenizer. For each encoding and dataset, we measure the
peak RSS (Resident Set Size) required by tokenization for input sizes of 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128 MiB.
We invoke tiktoken, tokenizers and gigatoken through their public APIs, which produce the output
as an array in memory. Recall that our algorithm streams an input file of characters to an output file of

7.3.1 Comparison with gigatoken

Recently, the release of the gigatoken tokenizer presents a parallelized tokenizer that achieves significant
throughput speedups over previous tokenizers [Rød26]. At a high level, gigatoken uses the following ideas
to dramatically speed up tokenization:

   1. Parallelism - Rød [Rød26] attempts to process a batch of files or a single file in parallel. In order to
       break up a single file, one can attempt certain heuristics to split up a large file based on known priors
       for pretokenization. For example, examining the tiktoken patterns, if we find a space between two
       words (a fairly common occurence), we can safely split a file at this space.

   2. Optimized Pretokenization - Using SIMD and many other tricks, Rød [Rød26] drastically reduces the
       workload of pretokenization, which is typically deferred to a regex engine.

   3. Caching - In order to avoid repeatedly merging the same pretoken, Rød [Rød26] maintains a cached
       mapping from pretokens to tokens. While some form of caching is already implemented in tiktoken,
       Rød [Rød26] uses a heavily optimized cache to improve performance.

A Omitted Proofs

We prove Lemma 5.3.

Proof of Lemma 5.3. We use the incremental BPE algorithm of Jiang and Gong [JG26]. Their algorithm
computes the last token of every prefix, whereas we require the first token of every suffix. The algorithm
and proof are almost identical, with some minor modifications to handle left-to-right tie-breaking in the
computation of first token ϕ rather than last token λ.

    We recap the algorithm of Jiang and Gong [JG26]. In preprocessing, they compute the following data
structures:

   1. A successor forest F where nodes are tokens τ and edges (τ, suc(τ )) where τ is produced by the merge
       rule (pre(τ ), suc(τ )) → τ . We call pre(τ ), suc(τ ) the predecessor and successor of τ . We replace this
       with a predecessor forest F where the nodes are tokens and edges (τ, pre(τ )). This requires O(m) time.

   2. For each token τ , compute the suffix-successor tree of τ , which is the induced subgraph of F containing
       tokens µ where raw(µ) is a suffix of raw(τ ). On each of these trees, compute a centroid decomposition
       (using e.g. Della Giustina, Prezza, and Venturini [DPV19]). Instead, we replace this with a prefix-
       predecessor tree, i.e. the induced subgraph of the predecessor forest F consisting of tokens µ where
       raw(µ) ⪯ raw(τ ). There are m tokens, each with at most t prefixes, so this requires O(mt) time.

          50
   3. Jiang and Gong [JG26] use an Aho–Corasick automaton to report the longest suffix of x⩽i that is a
       token. Instead, we scan right-to-left using an automaton on reversed token strings to report the longest
       prefix of x⩾i that is a token. This requires O(mt) preprocessing.

    We then process the input string x right-to-left. Assume that ϕ(x⩾j) is computed for j > i. We have
an analogue of Jiang and Gong [JG26] Prefix Last Token condition (see Definition 4.1), which we call the
Suffix First Token condition. That is, τ satisfies the Suffix First Token condition if it is an atomic token (i.e.
|raw(τ )| = 1) or satisfies the following:

   1. (Reachability) ϕ(x⩾i+|pre(τ)|) is in the subtree of the predecessor forest rooted at suc(τ ). This ensures
       that suc(τ ) is formed in the suffix x⩾i+|pre(τ)|.

   2. (Priority Dominance) If ϕ(x⩾i+|pre(τ)|) ̸= suc(τ ), let u be the unique child of suc(τ ) that lies on the
       path to ϕ(x⩾i+|pre(τ)|). Then, the rule producing τ has priority at least as high as the rule producing
       u. This ensures that τ is produced before suc(τ ) is consumed. Note that here we require priority to be
       at least as high, instead of strictly higher as in Jiang and Gong [JG26], due to left-to-right tie-breaking.

    Let ρ be the longest token such that raw(ρ) ⪯ x⩾i. Then raw(ϕ(x⩾i)) ⪯ raw(ρ). Analogously to Jiang
and Gong [JG26], one can show that on the prefix-predecessor tree of ρ, a vertex is on the path from xi to
ϕ(x⩾i) iff it satisfies the Suffix First Token condition, which can be checked in O(1) time. The idea then is to
start from the root xi and follow the path of children satisfying the Suffix First Token (SFT) condition. Since
we have constructed a centroid decomposition, we begin from the centroid. We first test the centroid’s SFT
condition. If it is valid, we search for a child satisfying the condition. We identify the valid child by binary
search, with children ordered by the left endpoints of their disjoint valid intervals, as in Jiang and Gong
[JG26], with predecessors and successors interchanged. Here children refer to the original prefix-predecessor
tree. If the centroid is valid but has no valid child, return it. Otherwise, either the centroid has a valid child,
in which case we remove the centroid and continue at the centroid of the remaining component containing
the valid child, or the centroid is invalid, in which case we remove it and continue at the centroid of the
remaining component containing its original-tree parent, as in Jiang and Gong [JG26]. This proceeds for at
most O(log t) steps, and searching for the right child requires O(log t) time, leading to O(log2 t) time.
