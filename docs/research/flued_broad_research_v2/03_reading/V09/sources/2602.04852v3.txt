                                                   Linear attention offers a computationally efficient yet expressive alternative to
                                                   softmax attention. However, recent empirical results indicate that the hidden state
                                                   of trained linear attention models often exhibits a low-rank structure, suggesting
                                                   that these models underexploit their capacity in practice. To understand this
                                                   phenomenon, we analyze how keys and values shape the rank of the recurrent state,
                                                   providing a theoretical perspective on memory utilization in linear attention. In
                                                   addition to these theoretical insights, we conjecture that low-rank states can be
                                                   substantially reduced after pre-training. To this end, we propose a hardware-aware
                                                   strategy to structurally prune the key and query matrices, reducing the state size
                                                  while retaining compatibility with existing fast kernels. We adapt several existing
                                                   pruning strategies to fit our framework and, building on our theoretical analysis,
                                                   propose a robust structured pruning method based on a rank-revealing QR decom-
                                                   position. Our empirical evaluations across model sizes show that the recurrent
                                                   state of pretrained linear attention models can often be halved with only a modest
                                                   increase in language modeling perplexity, while maintaining competitive perfor-
                                                   mance on downstream tasks. The code for this project can be found at https:

                                        1 INTRODUCTION

                                        Linear Attention (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Schlag et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2023; Gu
                                        & Dao, 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Dao & Gu, 2024; Yang et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2025) has emerged
                                        as an efficient alternative to softmax attention (Vaswani et al., 2017), enabling high-throughput
                                        chunkwise parallel training with linear time complexity and constant memory inference (Hua et al.,
                                        2022; Lingle, 2024; Yang et al., 2023). These efficiency gains have driven the development of
                                        large hybrid models (Blakeman et al., 2025; Qwen Team, 2026) which predominantly employ linear
                                        attention layers, interspersed with only a few softmax attention layers.

                                        Despite their impressive performance, prior work indicates that linear attention models underutilize
                                        their capacity in practice (Siems et al., 2026; Parnichkun et al., 2025). In particular, their matrix-
                                        valued hidden states exhibit a low-rank structure after training. This state acts, through the lens of
                                        linear associative memories (Ramsauer et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2025), as a store of sequence history.
                                        Thus, low effective rank indicates that the model might be using its memory inefficiently, effectively
                                        wasting capacity. In this work, we first provide a theoretical perspective on this phenomenon. We
                                        then investigate whether the hidden state of pretrained linear attention models can be reduced to
                                        improve inference speed and memory efficiency.

                                        Towards this end, we propose a structured pruning framework that relies on the observation that linear
                                        attention models are invariant under orthogonal transformations applied jointly to the queries and
                                        keys. Since the per-channel convolutions typically employed in recurrent models are not compatible
                                        with rotations (Chollet, 2017; So et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2022), we restrict the space of applicable
                                        transformations to key-channel selection methods. Within this space of transformations, we provide a
                                        reformulation of several established pruning strategies, including those based on parameter magnitude

2 THEORETICAL INSIGHTS

This section establishes the theoretical foundations for our analysis. We begin by introducing the
linear attention mechanism and its interpretation as an associative memory, before developing a
framework to characterize the effect of rank inefficiencies within this setting.

2.1 BACKGROUND ON LINEAR ATTENTION

In its simplest form, linear attention (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Schlag et al., 2021) arises by

replacing the exponential kernel in self attention with a linear one and discarding the normaliza-

tion (Vaswani et al., 2017). Let X ∈ RT,h be an input sequence that gets mapped to queries
Q = XWQ ∈ RT,dk , keys K = XWK ∈ RT,dk , and values V = XWv ∈ RT,dv via linear

projections. By the associativity of matrix multiplication, linear attention can then be expressed

recurrently as

                                        St = St−1 + vtkt⊤,                              (1)

where St ∈ Rdv,dk is a matrix-valued hidden state used to compute the output ot = Stqt, and
S0 = 0. Katharopoulos et al. (2020) provide a detailed derivation. The state St implements a linear

associative memory (Ramsauer et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2025) that is addressed using the queries.

Throughout this work, we will refer to the update rule of St as the sequence mixer.

The mechanism defined in Equation (1) can only write into the associative memory. DeltaNet (Schlag
et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2024) addresses this limitation by explicitly erasing old information correlated
with the current key before writing the new value to St,

                              St = St−1(I − βtktkt⊤) + βtvtk⊤t .                            (2)

Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2025a) further refines this mechanism by introducing a data-dependent
decay term.

By the nature of linear attention, the subspace spanned by the associative memory is governed by the
keys and values. Formally, for a family of linear attention models, the row and column spaces of the
associative memory satisfy

                      row St ⊆ span(k1, . . . , kt), col St ⊆ span(v1, . . . , vt).

                      rank St ≤ min (rank Kt, rank Vt) ≤ t,                                 (3)

where Kt = [k1, . . . , kt] and Vt = [v1, . . . , vt] are the matrices obtained by stacking the keys and
values, respectively. The proof is presented in Appendix F.2.

& Vershynin, 2007; Tropp, 2015; Vershynin, 2019; Ipsen & Saibaba, 2025), defined as

2.2.1 RANK UTILIZATION

While the effective rank measures the effective dimensionality of the state matrix, it does not capture
how efficiently a recurrent model uses its available memory. For a non-zero state S ∈ Rdv,dk with

maximum capacity d := min(dk, dv), we define its rank utilization as

                                        u(S)  :=  er(S)                 (5)
                                                         .
                                                     d

Rank utilization sets effective rank in relation to the theoretical maximum capacity and thus serves as
a measure for memory utilization in linear attention models. We identify two edge cases:

• Low Utilization (u ≪ 1): The memory suffers from rank collapse. Its energy is concentrated
  in a few principal components. Information is stored in an effectively low-dimensional
  subspace.

• High Utilization (u = 1): The memory is isotropic. Energy is distributed evenly across all
  dimensions.

which establishes a connection between the conditioning of the keys and the rank utilization of the
corresponding memory.

2.2.2 WHY DOES RANK UTILIZATION MATTER?

The previous sections introduced effective rank and rank utilization as measures of the isotropy of an
associative memory. We now connect these measures to noise sensitivity during readout. Intuitively,
a memory with a skewed singular spectrum responds more strongly to some directions than to others.
As a result, a small relative change in the query can lead to a larger relative change in the output.

To formalize this phenomenon, consider a perturbed query q˜ = q∗ + ε, ∥q∗∥2 = 1, with outputs
o∗ = Sq∗ and o = Sq˜, where we drop the time index for brevity. Define the relative error

3 THE PROPOSED PRUNING FRAMEWORK

Motivated by the above insights into the role of rank in linear attention, we propose a structured
pruning framework to reduce the per-head key dimension of linear attention models. Besides revealing
redundancy in pretrained linear attention models, our pruning strategy yields a strictly smaller hidden
state for larger throughput during inference.

3.1 INVARIANT TRANSFORMATIONS FOR LINEAR ATTENTION

Decomposing St row-wise reveals that the sequence mixer simulates dv parallel linear time-varying

(LTV) dynamical systems. Indeed, let h(ti) ∈ Rdk denote the transpose of the i-th row of St. Each
value channel i ∈ {1, . . . , dv} follows the vector-valued dynamics

ht(i) = Ath(t−i)1 + Btvt,i,                                      (8)

where the system matrices At = I − βtktkt⊤ and Bt = βtkt are shared across all value channels.
The readout is computed via contraction with the vector Ct = q⊤t .

The dynamical system in Equation (8) is invariant under a choice of basis in state-space (Chahine
et al., 2026; Chen, 1984). That is, every transformation

                                  (At, Bt, Ct) → (TAtT−1, TBt, CtT−1)

derived from an invertible matrix T ∈ GL(dk) leaves the input-output mapping invariant. If the
transformed system should also be expressible in the same functional form as the original recurrence,
requiring invertibility is not sufficient. Indeed, the transformed transition matrix

A˜ t = TAtT−1 = I − βt(Tkt)(T−⊤kt)⊤,

is generally not symmetric and can thus not be expressed as a delta-corrected linear recurrence. If
additionally T ∈ O(dk) is an orthogonal change of basis, this transformation can be absorbed to
preserve the structure of the recurrence:

Proposition 3.1 (Orthogonal Invariance of Sequence Mixing). Let T ∈ O(dk) be an orthogo-
nal matrix. Then the (Gated) DeltaNet attention mechanism is invariant under the simultaneous
transformation (kt, qt) → (Tkt, Tqt).

Our structured pruning framework, presented in the subsequent section, makes use of this result by
applying a semi-orthogonal transformation jointly to queries and keys.

3.2 STATE SIZE REDUCTION REQUIRES STRUCTURED PRUNING

Existing pruning methods for Large Language Models generally rely on unstructured or semi-
structured sparsity (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023; Sun et al., 2024). However, these methods do not
reduce the state dimension of the dynamical system, as they leave the query and key vectors dense
(see Figure 1). To address this issue, we focus on structured pruning.

Formally, given a semi-orthogonal matrix T ∈ Rdk′ ,dk with target dimension dk′ < dk, we apply it
jointly to the keys and queries before computing the dynamical system:

(kt, qt) → (Tkt, Tqt) ∀t = 1, . . . , T.

By Proposition 3.1, if dk′ = dk, this transformation yields an equivalent realization of the dynamical
system. Our goal is finding a T with d′k ≪ dk that entails a small error in the reduced system. A
natural framework for deriving such a transformation would be Principal Component Analysis (PCA).

The Convolution Constraint. To achieve actual wall-clock speedup, we aim to absorb T into
the weights WK and WQ. However, linear attention models typically apply causal convolutions
to queries and keys (Chollet, 2017; So et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023; Gu & Dao,
2024; Dao & Gu, 2024; Yang et al., 2024). While the sequence mixer is compatible with orthogonal
transformations (Proposition 3.1), depthwise convolutions are not. A dense semi-orthogonal matrix
T, such as one derived from PCA, would mix channels, misaligning them with their corresponding
convolution filters. Preserving the dynamics under such rotations would require converting the effi-
cient diagonal convolution filters into expensive dense matrices. Therefore, we effectively constrain
our framework to axis-aligned transformations. The case of general orthogonal matrices is discussed
in Appendix B.3.

3.3 AXIS-ALIGNED METHODS

To circumvent the above intricacies related to general dense rotations, we focus on transformations
that preserve the channel-wise convolution filters. Formally, this restricts transformations to axis-
aligned semi-orthogonal matrices: Let I = {i1, . . . , idk′ } ⊆ {1, . . . , dk} be a set of distinct indices
with cardinality dk′ < dk. We define the corresponding axis-aligned projection PI ∈ Rd′k,dk by
selecting the associated standard basis vectors as rows:

                                                                                                         ⊤

                                           PI := ei1 · · · eid′k .

This matrix is semi-orthogonal. Applying it corresponds to a structural pruning operation that selects
the subset of key-channels I and discards the rest. Crucially, this operation preserves the independence
of the remaining channels and can be obtained by slicing the corresponding columns from the
projection matrices WK and WQ, along with the corresponding entries of the convolution filters.

In the following section, we present a plethora of different strategies to select the index set I for
each head, ranging from simple magnitude-based heuristics to a novel rank-revealing approach.
By Proposition 3.1, every transformation must be applied to the queries and keys simultaneously.
Consequently, the proposed column selection mechanisms incorporate both queries and keys.

4 RELATED WORK

Rank Considerations. Recent empirical results show that the memory of linear attention models
often exhibits a low-rank structure (Siems et al., 2026; Parnichkun et al., 2025), indicating that they
do not manage their associative memory well. Our work builds on these observations, forcing the
model to operate in a lower-dimensional space with increased rank utilization and a minimal decrease
in effective rank (Ipsen & Saibaba, 2025).

More generally, rank collapse is a well-known phenomenon in transformers. Noci et al. (2022) reveal
that collapsed queries and keys hinder gradient flow at initialization, while Dong et al. (2021) show
that skip connections help alleviate it. In this work, we tie the rank of the queries and keys to that of
the hidden state in linear attention and show how a skewed spectrum of the associative memory can
amplify query noise during readout.

Pruning Methods. Conventional pruning methods are usually either unstructured or semi-
structured and thus require specialized kernels to realize sparsity-induced speedups (LeCun et al.,
1989; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023; Sun et al., 2024). SparseGPT (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023) frames
pruning as a local reconstruction problem. Sun et al. (2024) propose a simpler, gradient-free metric
based on the product of weight magnitudes and input activation norms. Several works have sought to
enhance this metric by re-incorporating gradients. GBLM (Das et al., 2023) and Pruner-Zero (Dong
et al., 2024) utilize gradients derived from full-model backpropagation to refine pruning scores.
Wanda++ (Yang et al., 2025b) introduces regional gradients to decrease the computational cost.

Recent works on quantizing linear attention variants, including Quamba (Chiang et al., 2025b;a),

6 DISCUSSION

The low-rank structure observed in pretrained linear attention states raises the question of whether
these models could use their memory capacity more effectively. To explore this question, we analyze
how keys and values shape the effective rank of the recurrent state and how this rank structure can
amplify the effect of query noise during memory readout. As a consequence, we show that structured
pruning of the keys and queries allows discarding at least half of the state while entailing only a minor
increase in perplexity. In addition to adapting several pruning strategies, we introduce a method with
a provable conditioning bound for the selected joint key-query activations. Our empirical evaluations
show that the resulting structured pruning framework can substantially reduce state size at scale.
While effective, we note that state reduction is generally not lossless, and competitive downstream
performance typically requires RFT after pruning. Our long-context results suggest that hybrid
models are more resilient on recall intensive tasks.

Future Work. As hybrid architectures become increasingly prominent, understanding the role of
linear layers in these models is an interesting direction for future work. Our long-context results
suggest that hybrids may be more resilient to state pruning than purely recurrent models, raising
the possibility of a division of labor between linear and softmax attention layers. Investigating how
these layers contribute to memory and retrieval could clarify the importance of recurrent capacity and
inform the design of more efficient hybrid architectures.

B.1 STRUCTURED PRUNING FROM THE TEST-TIME REGRESSION PERSPECTIVE

linear regression objective (Schlag et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2024), where the hidden state St acts as

proposed structured pruning strategy admits another interpretation, functioning as a feature selection

forces the fast weights to ignore the null space of T, acting as a form of regularization.

Algorithm and Update Rules. The DRRQR procedure (Algorithm 2) proceeds as follows:

       1. Initialization: Compute an initial factorization using standard QRCP. This provides a
          baseline Π, Ak, Bk, and Ck.

       2. Identification: Search for a pair of indices (i, j) such that ρij > f . Efficient search
          strategies maximize over j for fixed i, or simply identify the first valid pair.

       3. Update: If a valid pair is found:

            (a) Swap columns i and j + k of R and update the permutation Π accordingly.
           (b) Retriangularize the matrix R using Givens rotations to restore the upper-triangular

                structure of Ak.

B.3 ADAPTING CONVOLUTIONS TO GENERAL ROTATIONS

Handling the more general case of (semi-) orthogonal, non-axis-aligned transformations is more
intricate than the axis-aligned one (see Section 3.3). If one wishes to employ general orthogonal
transformations T ∈ O(dk) (such as those derived from PCA) to prune the sequence mixer, the
convolution layers must be adapted.

Depthwise convolutions operate independently on each channel. Rotating the input space via T
generally turns the exact filter matrices into dense matrices. To retain depthwise convolutions, we
approximate these rotated filters with diagonal matrices.

details.

Definition D.1 (effective rank). For a non-zero matrix A ∈ Rm,n, the effective rank is defined as:

                   er(A)                :=  ∥A∥2F  =    i σi2(A)  .
                                            ∥A∥22     σm2 ax(A)

Unlike the algebraic rank, which is discontinuous, the effective rank is a continuous function of the
matrix entries. This implies that small perturbations to the memory state St (e.g., from gradient noise

Proposition D.2 (Invariance under Transposition). The effective rank is invariant under transposition.
For any matrix A:

                                                 er(A) = er(A⊤).

invariant under unitary transformations and scalar multiplication. For any unitary matrices U, V

and non-zero scalar c ∈ R:
                                              er(cUAV⊤) = er(A).

algebraic rank:

                                     1 ≤ er(A) ≤ rank(A) ≤ min(m, n).

The lower bound is achieved if and only if A has rank 1. The upper bound is achieved if and only if
all non-zero singular values are equal.

E DYNAMICAL SYSTEMS PERSPECTIVE

In this section, we derive the DeltaNet recurrence rule from a continuous-time dynamical systems
perspective. We show that the sequence mixer can be interpreted as a discretization of a continuous
gradient flow minimizing a linear regression objective.

Continuous-Time Dynamics. Consider a time-continuous associative memory S(t) ∈ Rdv,dk
receiving a stream of keys k(t) ∈ Rdk and values v(t) ∈ Rdv . We define the instantaneous

regression loss at time t as:

                               L(S(t))  =  1   ∥v(t)   −  S(t)k(t)∥22  .
                                           2

The dynamics of the state S(t) are governed by the gradient flow minimizing this objective:

                               S˙ (t) = −∇SL(S(t))                                           (11)

                               = −(S(t)k(t) − v(t))k(t)⊤                                     (12)

                               = v(t)k(t)⊤ − S(t)k(t)k(t)⊤.                                  (13)

Equation (13) represents a linear time-varying (LTV) ordinary differential equation (ODE) of the

form S˙ (t) = S(t)A(t) + B(t), where A(t) = −k(t)k(t)⊤ is the state-transition matrix acting on
the right, and B(t) = v(t)k(t)⊤ is the input forcing term.

Euler Discretization. To obtain the discrete-time update rule employed by DeltaNet, we apply the
forward Euler method to Equation (13). Let ∆t be the step size, which corresponds to the gating
factor βt in the DeltaNet formulation. The discretization yields:

                               St − St−1   ≈ vtk⊤t − St−1ktkt⊤.
                                    βt

Rearranging the terms to solve for the next state St:

                               St = St−1 + βt(vtkt⊤ − St−1ktk⊤t )
                                  = St−1(I − βtktk⊤t ) + βtvtkt⊤.

This exactly recovers the DeltaNet update rule (Equation (2)).

System Stability. The stability of this dynamical system is determined by the spectral properties of
the transition matrix (I − βtktkt⊤). For the system to be stable (non-divergent), the singular values
of this operator must lie within the unit circle. This implies the condition |1 − βt∥kt∥22| ≤ 1. In
standard DeltaNet implementations, keys are normalized (∥kt∥2 = 1), and βt is the output of a

sigmoid function (βt ∈ (0, 1)), strictly satisfying the stability condition and ensuring the memory

decays appropriately over time.

In this section, we provide proofs of statements presented in the main paper.

F.1 PROOF OF THE EFFECTIVE-RANK BOUND

We prove Equation (4). Using the column-stacking convention of the main text, write the associative
memory as S = VK⊤, where V ∈ Rdv,T and K ∈ Rdk,T . Assume K has full row rank.

To handle potential misalignment between the subspaces of values and keys, we decompose the
values V into two orthogonal components relative to the row space of the keys K:

                                          V = V∥ + V⊥,

where the rows of V∥ lie in row(K), and the rows of V⊥ are orthogonal to it. Consequently,
V⊥K⊤ = 0, and the memory state simplifies to:

                                        S = (V∥ + V⊥)K⊤ = V∥K⊤.

Assume V∥ ≠ 0. We define the scalar quantity ν(V) appearing in the main text as the effective rank

of the projected values:

                                    ν(V)  :=  er(V∥)  =  ∥V∥∥2F  .
                                                         ∥V∥∥22

The quantity ν(V) also depends on K through this projection. Since multiplication by K⊤ is injective

on row(K), these assumptions ensure S ≠ 0. We now derive the lower bound for the effective rank
er(S) = ∥S∥2F /∥S∥22.

First, we bound the numerator (Frobenius norm) from below. We use the property ∥AB∥F ≥
∥A∥F σmin(B), which holds here because the rows of V∥ lie in col(K⊤):

F.2 PROOF OF ALGEBRAIC RANK

We first prove the following proposition:
Proposition F.1. Let S0 = 0 and St be a matrix satisfying the recursion

                          St = St−1(αtI − βtktkt⊤) + γtvtk⊤t

for some non-zero scalars αt, βt, γt and some vectors vt, kt. Then

               row St ⊆ span⟨k1, . . . , kt⟩ and col St ⊆ span⟨v1, . . . , vt⟩.

Consequently,

                                 rank St ≤ min (rank Kt, rank Vt)

where Kt = (k1, . . . , kt) and Vt = (v1, . . . , vt) are the matrices obtained by stacking the k- and
v-vectors, respectively.

On State Reduction in Linear Attention

Proof. We start by showing the first claim by induction. For t = 1, we have S1 = γ1v1k⊤1 . Consider
t > 1 and assume the claim is true for every s < t. Then

                                    St = St−1(αtI − βtktkt⊤) + γtvtkt⊤
                                        = αtSt−1 + (γtvt − βtSt−1kt)k⊤t
                                        = αtSt−1 + utk⊤t ,

where we defined ut := γtvt − βtSt−1kt. Next, we use that for two matrices A and B,

                           row(A + B) ⊆ span(row(A), row(B))

and thus

               row(αtSt−1 + utkt⊤) ⊆ span(row(αtSt−1), row(utk⊤t ))
                                          ⊆ span(row(St−1), kt)
                                          ⊆ span(k1, ..., kt).

This concludes the first claim.

For the second claim, we proceed analogously.

Indeed, we again show this claim by induction. The case t = 1 is clear. For any t > 1, we compute,
using col(AB) ⊆ col(A),

                           col(St) ⊆ col(St−1(αtI − βtktkt⊤), γtvtk⊤t )
                                    ⊆ col(St−1, γtvtkt⊤)
                                    ⊆ col(v1, . . . , vt).

This concludes the proof.

pruning the low-variance directions via PCA increases or maintains rank utilization. That is, for any

1 ≤ dk′ < dk:                           u(K′) ≥ u(K).
