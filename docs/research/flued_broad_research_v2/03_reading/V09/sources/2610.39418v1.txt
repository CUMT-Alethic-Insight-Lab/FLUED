                                                                                   ABSTRACT

                                                   Linear recurrences are emerging as practical alternatives to softmax attention in
                                                   large language models, appearing in both standalone and hybrid architectures.
                                                   However, despite their rapid emergence, low-precision inference remains much less
                                                   developed for these models than for softmax attention. During prefill, chunkwise-
                                                   parallel algorithms cast much of the recurrence as dense matrix multiplications,
                                                   suggesting a natural path to low-precision execution. Simply quantizing these
                                                   operations in their native form, however, can be fragile. In this work, we identify
                                                   functional invariances that preserve the full-precision computation while making
                                                   the operands more amenable to quantization. Building on this observation, we intro-
                                                   duce LinQuant, a framework for low-precision recurrent prefill based on seven such
                                                   invariances, including rotations, diagonal rescalings, and alternative placements of
                                                   recurrent decay and write factors. We derive when these transformations remain
                                                   valid across common recurrence families spanning linear, gated, and delta-rule
                                                   updates, and show that they provide complementary degrees of freedom for im-
                                                   proving low-precision accuracy. Finally, we translate this framework to production
                                                   by augmenting SSDi8, an existing low-precision prefill pipeline for Mamba-2, with
                                                   the applicable invariances. LinQuant improves perplexity at matched latency on
                                                   Mamba-2 1.3B, 2.7B, and 8B and Nemotron-H 8B, demonstrating the practical
                                                   utility of invariance-based quantization for recurrent models.

                                        1 INTRODUCTION

                                        Linear recurrences offer an efficient, sub-quadratic alternative to softmax attention for sequence
                                        modeling (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Dao & Gu, 2024; Yang et al., 2025; Team et al., 2025).
                                        They summarize past tokens in a fixed-size state, avoiding the growing key-value cache of softmax
                                        attention, and are increasingly used in both standalone and hybrid language models (NVIDIA, 2025;
                                        Amini et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2025; Beck et al., 2024). For prefill, these models are typically
                                        evaluated using chunkwise-parallel algorithms, which process blocks of tokens in parallel through
                                        dense matrix multiplications while propagating a recurrent state between them (Dao & Gu, 2024;
                                        Yang & Zhang, 2024). This mirrors the blockwise organization of efficient attention kernels such
                                        as FlashAttention and makes recurrent prefill naturally amenable to low-precision execution.

                                        Low-precision attention has been studied extensively, with optimized integer and floating-point
                                        kernels now forming a mature part of the Transformer inference stack (Zhang et al., 2025; Shah et al.,
                                        2024). In contrast, low-precision kernels for linear recurrences remain largely unexplored. Prior work
                                        has studied quantization of recurrent models (Xu et al., 2025; Chiang et al., 2025), largely targeting
                                        the projections and activations surrounding the Mamba-2 recurrence. SSDi8, on the other hand,
                                        executes the recurrent computation in low precision, demonstrating an efficient INT8 implementation
                                        specific to Mamba-2 (Kim et al., 2026). We instead study the space more generally, investigating
                                        low-precision computation across the design space of modern linear recurrent architectures.

Executing sequence mixers in low-precision can come at the cost of performance. One powerful lever
to mitigate this is functional invariances. Such transformations preserve the full-precision operator
while changing the numerics presented to a quantizer. Figure 12 in the appendix illustrates this princi-
ple through write-factor absorption. We distinguish two broad classes. Mixer-agnostic invariances act
on the residual stream or individual linear layers and do not depend on the algebra of the sequence
operator (Xiao et al., 2023; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Because these transformations are
largely independent of the mixer, they transfer naturally to recurrent architectures and have already
been adopted by methods such as MambaQuant and Quamba2 (Xu et al., 2025; Chiang et al., 2025).
Mixer-specific invariances instead rely on the sequence operator itself, and can be classified as either
mixer-external or mixer-internal. The former materialize as changes of basis and can be applied
outside of the sequence-mixer, while the latter are functions of a recurrence’s algorithmic realization
(Figure 1). Transformer PTQ has exploited such invariances within softmax attention, for example
through transformations of its query, key, and value representations (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al.,
2025). In contrast, the mixer-specific invariances of linear recurrences have yet to be characterized.

Linear recurrences present a qualitatively different invariance structure from softmax attention. Their
transformations must remain consistent with a state that is updated and carried across chunks, while
decay, gating, and delta-rule updates impose additional algebraic constraints. At the same time, these
same mechanisms create new degrees of freedom that have no direct attention analogue. We therefore
ask which attention-inspired invariances survive in recurrences, what new restrictions arise from the
recurrent computation, and what additional invariances are unique to linear recurrences. Our main
contributions are summarized as follows:

  • We introduce LinQuant, a framework for computing low-precision linear recurrences based on
    both mixer-agnostic and mixer-specific functional invariances, and characterize their applicability
    across common recurrent architectures.

  • Across a controlled set of linear recurrences, we show how recurrence structure, query-key
    normalization, quantization scale granularity, and state-storage basis determine which functionally
    equivalent realization is most robust to low precision.

  • We implement LinQuant in INT8 chunkwise kernels and fuse query–key transformations into
    SSDi8 without increasing measured prefill latency.

  • We apply LinQuant to production W4A8 models with INT8 recurrent states, improving over
    SSDi8 on Mamba-2 1.3B, 2.7B, and 8B and Nemotron-H 8B.

2.1 LINEAR RECURRENCES

Causal softmax attention (Vaswani et al., 2017) computes

                                     ot = V1⊤:t softmax K1:tqt/ Dk ,

from queries and keys qt, kt ∈ RDk and values vt ∈ RDv , where Dk and Dv denote the state
(key/query) and value dimensions, respectively. Linear attention replaces the nonlinear softmax

St ∈ RDv×Dk ,                     St = St−1 + vtk⊤t ,

                                                       ot = Stqt.

Modern linear recurrences extend this basic update with decay, write gating, and delta-rule correc-

common recurrence          St = St−1 Λt I − δ βt ktk⊤t + βt vtkt⊤,

where βt > 0 (βt ∈ (0, 2) for the delta rule), ∥Λt∥ ≤ 1, and δ ∈ {0, 1} is fixed for a given recurrence.

Equation 1 separates three structural choices.

Decay controls how the previous state is forgotten before new information is written. A scalar gate
Λt = αtI uses one decay rate per recurrent head (Dao & Gu, 2024; Yang et al., 2025), whereas a
diagonal gate Λt = diag(αt) allows the decay to vary across state channels (Yang et al., 2024b;
Team et al., 2025). Write gating scales the new association vtk⊤t . Delta correction (Schlag et al.,
2021; Yang et al., 2024c) removes the component of the existing state aligned with the current key
before writing the new association. Together, these choices organize the space of modern recurrences
as shown in Table 3 in Appendix B.1. We later instantiate this taxonomy as a controlled mixer zoo,
varying the recurrent update while holding the surrounding model architecture and training setup
fixed.

The chunkwise parallel forms in Section 2.2 expose a set of matrix multiplications and a state carried
between chunks. Importantly, these chunkwise forms are not unique: the same full-precision recur-
rence can be expressed through functionally equivalent realizations that expose different quantities
to low precision. LinQuant exploits this freedom. We first define the low-precision realization used
throughout the paper, then derive seven functional invariances: four axis transformations and three
absorptions. Table 1 summarizes their applicability; proofs are in Appendix C.

write factors within an equivalent chunkwise computation. Appendix E.2 studies sensitivity to the
stored-state and write-side quantization boundaries.

3.2 MIXER-EXTERNAL INVARIANCES: CHANGES OF BASIS

Both the key- and the value axis of the recurrent state St ∈ RDv×Dk expose a choice of basis which
leaves the full precision recurrence unchanged. We consider two such transformations along each
axis: orthogonal rotations and positive diagonal rescalings. Notably, changes of basis along the key
axis must remain compatible with the recurrent dynamics, whereas the value axis admits the same
equivariance across all recurrences in Equation (1).

(1/7) Key-side rotation. A joint orthogonal rotation of queries and keys,

                           (qt, kt) → (RQK qt, RQK kt), RQK ∈ O(Dk),

preserves query/key inner products and is a standard invariance of full precision softmax attention
exploited by rotation-based PTQ methods such as QuaRot and SpinQuant (Ashkboos et al., 2024;
Liu et al., 2025). In low precision, random rotations spread energy across channels, reducing outlier
concentration and improving quantization robustness.

For a linear recurrence, the invariance holds if RQK Λt = ΛtRQK (Nazari & Rusch, 2026), and
implicitly applies a change of basis to the state’s key axis, which transforms like St → StRQ⊤K .
Invariance thus holds with no decay or scalar decay. With diagonal decay, a general rotation does
not commute with Λt, so the recurrent state cannot be carried in a globally rotated basis. However,
we can still rotate queries and keys together after applying their decay factors when computing
within-chunk scores. The recurrent state remains unrotated (Appendix D.1).

In this work, we use random signed Hadamard rotations. Recurrent models may apply per-channel
causal convolutions and nonlinearities between their input projections and the recurrence, which
generally do not commute with a dense rotation. We thus apply RQK online after these operations.

(2/7) Key-side rescaling. The key axis also admits a reciprocal diagonal transformation,

                  (qt, kt) → (DQK qt, D−Q1K kt),                    DQK ≻ 0 diagonal,

which likewise preserves query-key inner products. Related rescalings underlie SmoothAttention in
QServe and, more broadly, methods such as SmoothQuant (Lin et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2023).

For a linear recurrence, the state implicitly transforms like St → StDQ−1K . Unlike a dense rotation,
DQK commutes with diagonal decay, making the transformation valid for linear writes under no,
scalar, or diagonal decay. It does not, however, preserve delta corrected writes.

DQK transports magnitude between queries and keys. In this work, we parameterize DQK = diag(d)
by dn = (maxt |kt,n|/ maxt |qt,n|)κ, where the maxima are computed over calibration tokens within

each  head.  The  exponent  κ  ∈  [0, 1]  controls  the  rescaling  strength.  Specifically,  κ  =  1  balances  the
                                                                                                    2
magnitudes of queries and keys. We leave other parametrizations to future work.

(3/7) Value-side rotation. The value axis admits an analogous orthogonal transformation,

                                        vt → RV vt, RV ∈ O(Dv).

In softmax attention, value rotations are used for KV-cache quantization (Liu et al., 2025). The
operator, however, is not invariant to value rotations, which have to be undone after the softmax.
Linear recurrences exhibit the same equivariance. Intuitively, this is because the recurrence acts
independently along the value axis, which is implicitly rotated like St → RV St.

We note that, compared to softmax attention, the recurrent setting reveals another degree of freedom.
The state can, in between chunks, either be stored in its canonical basis, Q(St), or in a rotated basis,
Q(RV St). These realizations are equivalent in full precision but expose different representations to
Q. Rotated storage is what the rotation produces by itself, whereas canonical storage returns each
chunk’s write to the original basis before it joins the carried state. This comes at the cost of an inverse
transform per chunk, and keeps the state in the basis whose per-channel scales fit it.

(4/7) Value-side rescaling. Finally, the value axis admits the positive diagonal analogue

                                     vt → DV vt, DV ≻ 0 diagonal.

The recurrent state transforms accordingly as St → DV St, with the inverse rescaling applied to the
recurrence output before downstream normalization or other operations that do not commute with it.

3.3 MIXER-INTERNAL INVARIANCES: ABSORPTIONS

The first four invariances discussed in the previous section change the basis along the two axes of the
recurrent state. The remaining three redistribute multiplicative factors within a chunkwise parallel
implementation of the recurrence. In particular, decay and write factors can often be divided between
the two sides of a matrix product without changing the full-precision operator.

(5/7) Scalar-decay absorption. For scalar decay, the tail-decay Γ[→i] can be split between the value
and key sides of the state write:

       H[i] = ((Γ[→i] )η ⊙ (B[i]V[i]))⊤((Γ→ [i] )1−η ⊙ K[i]),                    η ∈ [0, 1].

Thus, η continuously interpolates between equivalent realizations of the same write. At η = 0, all tail
decay remains on the keys, which we call key-side decay absorption. At η = 1, it moves entirely
to the values, giving value-side decay absorption, the endpoint used by SSDi8 (Kim et al., 2026).
Intermediate values split the decay between the two sides. The same split applies to delta-rule writes,
replacing B[i]V[i] by the corresponding pseudo-values F[i].

(6/7) Diagonal-decay absorption. Diagonal decay varies across state channels and therefore cannot,
in general, be transferred entirely to the value side. Nevertheless, we can factor each token’s decay
into a scalar magnitude and a relative channelwise pattern. This decomposition is not unique: any
positive scalar factor yields an exact redistribution. Here, we use the channelwise maximum, and
leave alternative choices to future work.

Here, η moves the magnitude of the diagonal decay. At η = 0, the standard key-side form is
recovered. At η = 1, each token’s scalar decay magnitude moves to the value side, while the relative
channel-dependent decay necessarily remains on the key.

           H[i] = (Bξ[i]V[i])⊤(Γ→ [i] ⊙ B[1i−] ξK[i]),                     ξ ∈ [0, 1].

The endpoints ξ = 1 and ξ = 0 place the write factor entirely on the value and key sides, respectively.

Together, the four changes of basis and three absorptions define seven functional degrees of freedom
for realizing the same recurrent computation. Their applicability depends on the recurrence structure,
while their numerical behavior depends on the resulting low-precision representation, which we
explore in the following section.

4 EXPERIMENTS

In this section, we first evaluate how the invariances discussed in Section 3 affect quantization
error in a controlled model suite (Section 4.1). We then translate these insights to a production
setting, evaluating quality on pretrained Mamba-2 and Nemotron-H models and latency on Mamba-2
(Section 4.2).

4.1 ANALYZING LINQUANT

Model suite. To isolate the impact of each component that comprises the linear recurrence, we set up
a controlled mixer zoo. All models share the same backbone and training recipe. It consists of Linear
Attention, Mamba-2, GLA, DeltaNet, Gated DeltaNet, and KDA at 340M and 1.3B parameters,
trained with 15B and 100B tokens of SlimPajama, respectively. The 340M suite furthermore contains
models that vary write gating βt and QK normalization. Appendix B.1 gives the full setup.

Quantization settings. We evaluate how the identified invariances affect quantization error using
WikiText-2 test NLL, comparing against full precision and a naive quantizer without invariance
transforms. We use the signed Hadamard rotations and diagonal rescalings discussed in Section 3.2,
calibrated using WikiText-2. Several normalized decaying and delta-rule models already incur little
quantization error at INT8 with per-channel value scales (Table 7). We therefore use INT6 in the
controlled study to amplify quantization effects and make the impact of the invariances easier to
distinguish. INT6 uses a restricted integer range within our INT8 kernels; the production experiments
use INT8 recurrent computation. The end-of-chunk states are passed in FP32, while quantizing a
copy to an INT8 readout. Appendix B.1 gives calibration details, and Appendix E reports INT8,
INT4, 1.3B, and zero-shot results.

Key-side transformations transfer. Across every reported Mamba-2 and Nemotron-H model, RQK
improves perplexity over the matched SSDi8 setting, while DQK provides an additional gain in
most configurations (Table 2). Both DQK and RQK are fused into the operand-preparation pass.
Query–key rotation adds no measurable end-to-end prefill latency on the evaluated Mamba-2 models
(Figure 5a), while fused rescaling leaves the measured Mamba-2 1.3B layer latency unchanged
(Table 14). Full results are reported in Appendix F. The SSDi8 pipeline already uses per-channel
value scales, matching the controlled setting in which RV and DV provide little benefit. Rotated
state storage performs worse, while canonical storage does not improve over the unrotated baseline
(Appendix F.1). Since canonical RV adds overhead (Figure 5b), we apply neither value-side
transformation in the production configuration.
Kernels. The optimized SSDi8 kernels quantize operands once and reuse their INT8 representations
across multiple contractions. Kim et al. (2026) place scalar decay on the value side, motivated by
concerns that key-side decay would require per-position scales and materializing per-head keys. Our
evaluated key-side configurations achieve comparable perplexity to the value-side configurations
(Appendix F), and per-head decay can be applied in GPU registers without materializing the expanded
tensor (Appendix D.2). Their runtime also depends on operand reuse. Moving decay onto an operand
after its INT8 representation has been formed requires rereading and requantizing the floating-point
tensor. The cost therefore depends on which operand is affected, its dimension, and how often its
quantized representation is reused. Figure 5c shows that value-side absorption is faster for the evalu-
ated Mamba-2 shapes, although the gap narrows and can reverse as the value dimension Dv increases.
We therefore retain value-side absorption for its lower runtime on the target Mamba-2 geometries.

5 CONCLUSION

In this work, we introduce LinQuant, a framework that exploits seven functional invariances for low-
precision linear recurrences, and characterize their applicability across a wide range of recurrent mix-
ers. These consist of mixer-external changes of basis and mixer-internal absorptions, namely alterna-
tive allocations of decay and write factors between operands. Our controlled study shows their benefits
depend on recurrence structure, QK normalization, scale granularity, and the basis used to quantize the
state. Notably, changes of basis and write-gate allocation are particularly effective under coarse scales.
These findings transfer to production Mamba-2 and Nemotron-H models, where LinQuant improves
quantization quality. Moreover, on the evaluated Mamba-2 models, query–key rotation adds no mea-
surable prefill latency. Although our implementation focuses on integer prefill, the introduced invari-
ances provide a broader design space (e.g. floating-point) for future low-precision implementations.

A EXTENDED RELATED WORK

Linear recurrent and hybrid language models. A growing line of work replaces softmax attention
with recurrent sequence mixers whose state size (Parnichkun et al., 2025) and per-token decode
cost do not grow with context length. This is achieved by implicitly summarizing past tokens in a
fixed-size hidden state, instead of explicitly in a KV-cache (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Schlag et al.,
2021; Yang et al., 2024b). Selective state-space models such as Mamba and Mamba-2 introduce
input-dependent recurrent dynamics and hardware-efficient implementations (Gu & Dao, 2024; Dao
& Gu, 2024).

More expressive recurrences modify how information is retained and written into the hidden state.
DeltaNet corrects stored associations by removing existing associations from its state (Yang et al.,
2024c), while Gated DeltaNet combines this correction mechanism with scalar forgetting (Yang
et al., 2025), and KDA uses channelwise decay (Team et al., 2025). Preconditioned DeltaNet further
incorporates curvature-aware updates (Tumma et al., 2026), while DeltaProduct enhances the state
transition operator (Siems et al., 2025). In a recent development, large-scale hybrid models combine
recurrent and softmax attention layers (Team et al., 2025; Blakeman et al., 2025).

Our work studies how the structure of these different recurrences enables and constrains functional
invariances. We then leverage these invariances for post-training quantization (PTQ).

PTQ of softmax Transformers. Weight-only PTQ methods such as GPTQ and AWQ reduce
quantization error through reconstruction-based compensation or channel protection (Frantar et al.,
2023; Lin et al., 2024). For joint weight and activation quantization, SmoothQuant transfers activation
range into weights (Xiao et al., 2023), while QServe combines quantization with efficient serving (Lin
et al., 2025). Rotation-based methods redistribute concentrated energy across channels: QuIP# uses
Hadamard incoherence processing for weight quantization (Tseng et al., 2024), QuaRot extends
rotations to weights, activations, and the KV cache (Ashkboos et al., 2024), and SpinQuant learns
rotations from calibration data (Liu et al., 2025). LinQuant extends the study of functional invariances
to linear recurrences.

Quantization of recurrent language models. Quamba develops an 8-bit PTQ recipe around sensitive
selective-scan features and activation outliers (Chiang et al., 2024). MambaQuant uses variance-
aligned rotations (Xu et al., 2025), while Quamba2 extends PTQ across Mamba and Mamba-2 at
multiple precisions (Chiang et al., 2025).

SSDi8 targets low-precision execution inside the chunkwise Mamba-2 computation (Kim et al., 2026).
It reuses quantized keys and queries, absorbs decay into values, and carries an INT8 state, all to
maintain a persistent INT8 computational path. This decay placement is the value-side endpoint of
our scalar-decay absorption family. Our production pipeline builds on SSDi8 by adding state-axis
rotations and rescalings (Section 4.2).

Recurrent-state compression beyond quantization. Chahine et al. (2025) prune the hidden state
during training by leveraging the free choice of key-axis basis, which we identify as invariance (1).
Similarly, Nazari & Rusch (2026) leverage the free choice of basis to prune the state of pretrained
linear recurrences. Low-rank and sparse recurrent connectivity has also been studied outside language
modeling (Tumma et al., 2024). In LinQuant, we instead retain state dimensions and use equivalent
representations to improve quantization, addressing a complementary source of recurrent efficiency.

The weighted tile is quantized and clipped before the INT8 tensor-core contraction. Programs
for heads in the same group read the same underlying key tile and apply their own weights, so
head-specific values never need to be stored as an expanded floating-point tensor.

keys and applies head-specific decay in registers (Appendix D.2). The remaining difference is in the

Figure 15 shows that the other stages are unchanged.
